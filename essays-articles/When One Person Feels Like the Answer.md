### **A Note to the Reader**

This book is educational, not a diagnosis or a substitute for individualized mental health care. Limerence is a developing area of research rather than a formal diagnostic category, and this book distinguishes direct limerence findings from evidence drawn from neighboring fields such as attachment, obsessive-compulsive behavior, emotion regulation, romantic relationships, grief, and behavioral change.

The practical frameworks in these pages are designed to help you observe patterns, make more deliberate choices, and communicate more honestly. They are not validated diagnostic instruments or standalone clinical treatments. When obsessive thinking or compulsive behavior is severely impairing your life, when trauma or depression is intense, when you are experiencing self-harm or suicide urges, or when anyone’s safety is at risk, seek appropriate professional or crisis support rather than relying on self-guided reading alone.

Most importantly, this book does not treat intensity as a moral failure. It also does not use psychological explanations to erase another person’s autonomy. Both truths will remain present throughout: your internal experience deserves understanding, and your behavior still has consequences.

### **How to Use This Book**

You do not need to complete exercises after every chapter or analyze every thought you have. This is a book to read first and use second.

Six recurring frameworks will appear throughout the manuscript: the Hope Loop, Reality–Story–Unknowns, the Five Displacements, the Trigger-to-Need Map, Impact Before Story, and Distributed Need Fulfillment. They are intentionally few. Each is meant to organize a larger body of explanation rather than replace it.

Read in order if you can. Parts I and II establish the vocabulary used later for behavior change, relationships, boundaries, and repair. If a chapter becomes emotionally overwhelming, stopping is allowed. Understanding a pattern is not improved by forcing yourself through material you are not ready to process.

> ⚠️ **Crisis & Boundaries Fast-Track:** If you are currently experiencing unwanted pursuit, feeling unsafe, or if your behavior is crossing into coercion or stalking, please turn directly to **Chapter 10: Boundaries, Responsibility, and When Intensity Stops Being Romantic**.

### **Core Frameworks Quick Guide**

*These frameworks are evidence-informed organizing tools. Return to them when you need a simpler way to locate yourself inside a complicated pattern.*

**1. The Hope Loop**
`Uncertainty → Salience → Interpretation → Fantasy → Urge → Checking/Contact → Relief → Renewed Uncertainty`
*Use it to understand why short-term relief can keep long-term uncertainty active.*

**2. Reality–Story–Unknowns**
*   **Reality:** What was observed or explicitly said?
*   **Story:** What meaning have you added?
*   **Unknown:** What information is genuinely unavailable?
*Use it when interpretation starts feeling identical to fact.*

**3. The Five Displacements**
`Time · Attention · Attunement · Truth · Future`
*Use it to examine what has shifted in an existing relationship without reducing the question to whether one technical line was crossed.*

**4. Trigger-to-Need Map**
`Trigger → Interpretation → Body State → Urge → Behavior → Immediate Payoff → Longer-Term Cost → Need`
*Use it to find the actual places where a repetitive sequence can change.*

**5. Impact Before Story**
`What happened? → What did it cost? → What will change? → Then, what explains the pattern?`
*Use it when psychological explanation is in danger of arriving before accountability.*

**6. Distributed Need Fulfillment**
Spread important needs across a wider system of self, relationships, values, competence, community, pleasure, creativity, support, and meaningful activity.
*Use it when one person’s attention has begun carrying too much of your emotional life.*

### **Introduction: When One Person Starts to Feel Like the Answer**

It is 8:43 on a Tuesday night, and Riley is trying not to check the phone again.

There is nothing unusual about the room. Morgan is on the other end of the couch, half watching a show and half scrolling through something on a tablet. A laundry basket is still sitting by the stairs. Someone needs to take the dog out before bed. Work starts early tomorrow.

Nothing is happening.

That is part of what makes the feeling so difficult to explain.

Avery has not replied.

Riley sent the last message a little after five. It was not a confession. It was not an argument. It was barely even important. A joke. A question. Something ordinary.

Three and a half hours later, Riley has thought about it dozens of times.

Maybe the joke landed strangely. Maybe Avery is out with someone. Maybe the last conversation felt different to Avery than it did to Riley. Maybe Riley has been imagining the entire closeness between them. Maybe Avery is deliberately creating space.

Or maybe Avery is simply making dinner.

That possibility occurs to Riley, too. It just does not stay convincing for very long.

The phone remains face down. Riley knows there is no useful reason to check it. If Avery has answered, the phone will still contain the answer in ten minutes. If Avery has not answered, checking will not make a message appear.

Still, the urge grows. There is a strange physical quality to it. Restlessness. Pressure. A sense that there is something unfinished nearby.

Morgan laughs at something on the television. Riley laughs a second too late.

“You okay?”
“Yeah. Just tired.”

That is not exactly a lie. But it is nowhere close to the truth.

The truth is that another person has begun to affect the weather inside Riley’s mind. A warm interaction with Avery can make the entire day feel bright. A short reply can flatten it. Silence can fill hours. One compliment can be carried around for a week. An ambiguous moment can be replayed until it begins to feel less like a memory and more like evidence in a case no one else knows is being tried.

Riley does not want to be dramatic about this. That is another part of the problem. From the outside, very little has happened. From the inside, almost everything has.

**When a feeling starts organizing your life**

People think intensely about other people all the time. We fall in love. We get crushes. We miss people who have left us. We wonder whether a first date went well. We imagine futures with people we barely know. We grieve relationships that never became what we hoped they would become.

None of this is automatically a psychological problem. Human beings are built for attachment. We are affected by being wanted, ignored, loved, rejected, chosen, touched, remembered, and understood. Romantic attraction can be distracting. Early love can be consuming. Rejection can make otherwise sensible people reread texts at midnight.

The question is not whether you have ever behaved irrationally while wanting someone. Most of us have. The more useful question is what begins to happen when one particular person takes on so much emotional importance that your attention, mood, hope, self-worth, or ability to feel settled starts depending on what happens next with them.

That is the territory of this book. The word we will use for much of it is *limerence*.

Psychologist Dorothy Tennov introduced the term in the 1970s to describe an intense state of romantic preoccupation centered on a powerful desire for reciprocation. Since then, the word has traveled much farther in popular culture than the research field studying it. Only recently have researchers begun developing stronger tools for measuring the experience itself.

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> A 2025 study creating the Limerence Questionnaire found two broad dimensions in participants’ experiences: an intense need for attachment and a pattern of neglecting oneself or other responsibilities as attention became concentrated on the person of interest. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*
> 
> A larger 2026 study of people reporting impairing limerence described the experience as chronic, intrusive, and involuntary. In a smaller real-time portion of that research, limerent thoughts appeared in roughly half of sampled waking moments and shifted along with emotional states, especially sadness and loneliness. *(Evans et al., 2026)*

Those findings matter. They tell us this can be more than a poetic name for having a terrible crush. They do not tell us that limerence is a settled diagnosis, a single disorder, or a problem with one known cause. It is not. The science is still developing.

That distinction will matter throughout this book because limerence attracts explanations almost as easily as the limerent mind attracts interpretations. You may have heard that it is anxious attachment. Or childhood trauma. Or dopamine addiction. Or OCD. Or ADHD hyperfocus. Or a nervous system seeking safety.

Each of those ideas may contain something useful for some people. None deserves to become a universal explanation simply because it sounds convincing. This book will not ask you to trade one fantasy for another. If we are going to examine the stories your mind creates about another person, we should be equally careful about the stories we create about your mind.

**The person and the job they have been given**

One of the central ideas of this book is simple: Sometimes the problem is not only how badly you want someone. It is how many jobs that person has started doing inside your emotional life.

They may represent romance. But perhaps they also represent proof that you are still desirable. Proof that your life can change. Proof that you are interesting. Proof that somebody finally understands you. A future. An escape. A second chance. A reason to wake up excited. A way to feel less lonely. A way to stop thinking about yourself. A way to become a version of yourself you like better.

This does not mean your feelings are fake. It does not mean the other person is imaginary. It certainly does not mean you would feel exactly the same about any random person who happened to show you attention.

Avery may truly be funny. Avery may truly understand Riley in ways Morgan does not. There may be real attraction. Real affection. Real compatibility. Real chemistry. Reality matters. But reality is not always the only thing carrying the feeling.

A person can be real and still become symbolic. They can be themselves and also become the doorway through which you imagine another life. When that happens, losing the person may begin to feel like losing far more than a person.

You are not only afraid of losing Avery. You may be afraid of losing the future Avery represents. The version of yourself you become around Avery. The feeling of possibility. The answer to a question you had not realized you were asking.

That is why simply saying, “They’re not that special,” usually does not help. Maybe they are special. But the intensity often cannot be understood by studying them alone. We also have to understand what has gathered around them.

**Compassion is not the same thing as permission**

There is another promise I want to make before we go any further. In my clinical work and my own life, I have seen how easily intense longing can become a source of silent shame. This book will not shame you for having needs.

Wanting reassurance does not make you weak. Wanting to be chosen does not make you pathetic. Wanting touch, attention, recognition, excitement, closeness, or emotional safety does not make you too much. These are deeply human needs.

Many people who become trapped in limerence have already spent enough time telling themselves they are ridiculous, obsessive, embarrassing, selfish, disloyal, broken, or impossible to love. Shame rarely produces the kind of change people hope it will. More often, it makes the pattern harder to look at.

But compassion has an important limit. Understanding why you did something does not erase what the behavior did to somebody else.

You can have an attachment wound and still cross a boundary. You can be terrified of abandonment and still frighten someone with repeated contact. You can be lonely and still neglect your partner. You can feel a desperate need for reassurance and still place an unfair emotional burden on another person. You can have no intention of hurting anyone and still create hurt.

A mature understanding of limerence has to hold both truths. Your experience deserves compassion. Other people’s experience of your behavior deserves reality. We are not going to choose one at the expense of the other.

Later in the book, we will spend considerable time with the people who absorb the weight of limerence: partners who feel another relationship quietly entering their home, and people who become idealized so intensely that they no longer feel free to be ordinary. That may be uncomfortable. It is supposed to be useful, not punishing. There is a difference.

Accountability asks, *What happened, and what can I do differently?*
Shame says, *What happened proves what kind of person I am.*
Only one of those gives you somewhere to go.

**This is not a diagnosis**

You will notice that I often use phrases such as *may*, *can*, *for some people*, and *one possible explanation*. That is intentional. Limerence is still an emerging research area. The available literature is growing, but it is small compared with the evidence base for topics such as depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, attachment, or anxiety.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> A recent scoping review described the direct research literature on limerence as extraordinarily limited. *(Springer, 2024)*

That means we need two kinds of honesty. First, we should take the experience seriously. Second, we should not pretend that psychologists have already solved it.

Some parts of this book will come directly from limerence research. Other parts will draw from better-developed fields such as attachment science, rumination, obsessive-compulsive behavior, emotion regulation, romantic attraction, learning, relationship psychology, and behavioral change. And some of the tools in this book are frameworks created to help you organize those ideas. I will not present those frameworks as medical discoveries. They are maps. A map can be useful without being the territory.

You are also not expected to diagnose yourself while reading. There is no quiz at the end of this introduction that will tell you whether you are officially limerent. For some readers, that word will fit perfectly. For others, it will fit partly.

You may recognize a painful mix of attraction, anxious attachment, obsessive thinking, loneliness, fantasy, grief, or dissatisfaction with your current life. That is enough to keep reading. The goal is not to earn a label. The goal is to understand what your mind has been doing and recover more choice about what happens next.

**Recovery does not mean becoming cold**

People sometimes approach this work with a private fear. *If I get better, will I stop caring? Will I have to admit the connection meant nothing? Will I become emotionally flat? Will I lose the part of myself that is capable of loving this deeply?*

No. That is not the goal.

Intensity is not the enemy. Love is not the enemy. Fantasy is not the enemy. Attachment is not the enemy. The problem is rigidity—when your emotional world becomes so tightly organized around one person that there is no room left for uncertainty, ordinary life, other relationships, or your own values.

Recovery is not learning how to stop wanting. It is learning how to want without disappearing. It is learning how to feel an urge without automatically obeying it. How to tolerate an unanswered question. How to recognize the difference between what happened and what you fear it means. How to let another person be important without making them responsible for your entire emotional stability. How to grieve what cannot be yours without making grief evidence that you cannot survive. How to build a life that contains enough sources of belonging, purpose, pleasure, attachment, novelty, and self-respect that one human being no longer has to carry all of them.

That is where we are going. But first, we need to understand the experience itself. Because Riley’s problem is not simply that Riley thinks about Avery too much. The deeper problem is that every thought has started asking the same question: *What does this mean about whether Avery wants me?* And every possible answer has started meaning too much.

### **Chapter 1: The Person You Cannot Stop Thinking About**

Avery eventually replies at 9:07.

*Sorry, busy night. That made me laugh though.*

Riley’s body relaxes before Riley has even finished reading. There it is. Relief. Not excitement exactly. Not yet. First, relief.

The story Riley had been building for the last few hours collapses almost instantly. Avery was not pulling away. Riley had not ruined anything. The joke was not embarrassing. The connection is still alive.

Then comes the second feeling. Hope.

*That made me laugh though.*
Why add *though*? And why apologize for being busy? Maybe Avery wanted Riley to know the delay was not personal. Maybe Avery had been thinking about replying. Maybe—

Riley catches the thought. Then follows it anyway. Within minutes, the painful uncertainty has changed form, but it has not actually disappeared.

This is one of the confusing things about limerence. The answer does not always end the question. Sometimes it simply gives the mind better material.

**When attraction becomes occupation**

It is difficult to draw a clean line between strong attraction and limerence because ordinary romantic attraction can be intense. People who are newly in love often think about each other a great deal. Research on romantic love has described intrusive thinking, focused attention, idealization, craving for emotional union, and strong shifts in mood as common features of passionate attachment.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Research on the reward and motivation systems involved in intense romantic love shows that passionate attachment shares brain systems related to reward and craving. *(Fisher et al., PubMed Central)*

So if you have spent an afternoon replaying a first kiss, there is no need to turn it into a clinical event. If you checked your phone five times after a promising date, you are in very crowded company. If you have imagined a future with someone you barely know, you are still human.

Intensity alone is not enough. What starts to matter is how much flexibility remains.
* Can your attention return to the rest of your life?
* Can you feel disappointed without becoming destabilized?
* Can you receive unclear information without spending hours trying to force it into certainty?
* Can you continue relating to the person as they actually are, including evidence that conflicts with what you hope?
* Can you allow their attention to rise and fall without your sense of worth rising and falling with it?
* Can you choose not to check? Can you stop?

For someone in a limerent state, those questions may become increasingly difficult to answer.

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> The 2025 research that produced the Limerence Questionnaire separated the experience into two related dimensions. One reflected intense attachment and longing for reciprocation. The other reflected neglect of oneself, other relationships, or responsibilities because attention had shifted so heavily toward the person. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*

That second piece matters. It moves us away from the idea that limerence is simply “loving too much.” The issue is not only what you feel. It is what the feeling begins to reorganize.

Maybe your work still gets done, but only after losing an hour to scrolling. Maybe you remain physically present with your partner while mentally conducting another relationship. Maybe you cancel plans because the person might become available. Maybe sleep starts depending on whether a conversation feels settled.

Maybe you are still functioning on paper. But there is less of you available for everything else. That is an important form of cost.

**Ruminative hope**

One phrase will help us describe what is happening: *Ruminative hope*.

Rumination means repetitive thinking that circles the same material without producing much new understanding. Hope gives that thinking a direction. Put them together and you get a particular kind of mental occupation:

*Maybe this means something. Maybe there is still a chance. Maybe I need one more piece of information. Maybe the next interaction will finally tell me.*

The mind keeps working because the problem feels solvable. This is different from thinking about somebody simply because you enjoy remembering them. Ruminative hope is investigative. It looks for evidence. It treats details as clues.

A pause before answering. A change in punctuation. Where they sat. How long they hugged you. Whether they viewed your story. Whether they remembered something small. Whether they acted differently around you than around someone else. Whether “we should do that sometime” meant we should actually do that sometime.

The trouble is that human behavior is noisy. People are inconsistent. Warm people are warm. Busy people become slow at texting. Some people flirt without wanting a relationship. Some people feel attraction they never intend to pursue. Some people care deeply and still say no. Some people do not know what they want. Some people give mixed signals because their own feelings are mixed. Some people are simply having a Tuesday.

When you are emotionally invested, all of this ordinary ambiguity becomes difficult to leave alone. You begin to mine it. And because the evidence is rarely complete, interpretation fills the gap.

> 💡 **Micro-Observation Prompt:**
> *Before continuing, take 10 seconds to identify: What is one piece of ordinary ambiguity you have recently mined for evidence?*

**Why uncertainty matters so much**

We often assume attraction should grow when we receive positive information. Sometimes it does. But uncertainty can have a strange effect on attention.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> In a well-known 2011 experiment, women who were uncertain about how much several men liked them reported thinking about those men more and feeling more attracted to them than participants who knew the men definitely liked them. The researchers proposed that increased thinking helped explain the effect. *(Whitchurch, Wilson, & Gilbert, 2011)*

That does not prove uncertainty causes limerence. It does show something important about the human mind: not knowing can keep a person mentally active. An unanswered question has unfinished business built into it.

Think about the difference between these three situations:
1. Someone clearly tells you they are not interested. *Painful.*
2. Someone clearly tells you they want to date you and begins building a relationship with you. *Exciting.*
3. Someone behaves warmly, perhaps flirtatiously, but never gives you enough information to know what the relationship is becoming.

That third situation leaves the door open. The mind can keep returning to it because possibility remains alive. For some people, rejection eventually closes the loop. For others, even rejection can be converted into uncertainty: *Maybe they are scared. Maybe the timing is wrong. Maybe they said no because they are trying to do the responsible thing. Maybe they will change their mind.*

This is why uncertainty is not always something the other person is deliberately creating. Sometimes the outside situation is genuinely unclear. Sometimes the limerent mind keeps creating uncertainty after reality has become more definite than it wants to accept. Those are not the same thing. We will return to that distinction later.

For now, notice how hope changes the meaning of ambiguity. An unanswered message is not merely an unanswered message. It becomes a question about the future. A smile is not merely a smile. It becomes evidence that the future may still be possible. And when the imagined future begins carrying enough emotional weight, every small signal about it begins to matter.

**The imagined future partner**

Limerence is often described as intense focus on another person. That is true, but incomplete. Much of the experience can actually be focused on the future. Not the life you are living. The life that might happen.

There is a version of Avery in Riley’s actual life. That Avery has a job, irritating habits, an ex who still causes occasional drama, strong opinions about food, a tendency to avoid difficult conversations, and a calendar that does not revolve around Riley.

Then there is another Avery. The Avery of possibility.

That Avery represents spontaneous weekends. Being understood without explaining everything. A more exciting sex life. Long conversations at two in the morning. A relationship that somehow avoids all the disappointments Riley already knows how to have.

In imagination, Avery does not have to leave socks on the floor. Avery does not have to get sick on vacation. Avery does not have to become stressed about money. Avery does not have to become boring on a Thursday in February. The imagined relationship has not failed these tests because it has not taken them.

This does not make the fantasy foolish. Every future begins partly in imagination. People have to imagine dating somebody before they date them. They have to imagine a shared life before they build one. But imagination has an unfair advantage over reality. It can select its evidence. It can skip the tedious scenes. It can repair conflict instantly. It can give people motives they have never stated. It can make compatibility feel proven before compatibility has had to survive anything difficult.

This is why wanting someone intensely is not the same as knowing them deeply. Sometimes we know someone very well and still become limerent. Sometimes the person is a close friend, coworker, ex, mentor, neighbor, or longtime acquaintance. The issue is not necessarily lack of information. It is what the mind does with the information it has.

A real person can become the foundation for an imagined future that grows much larger than the available evidence. And once the future matters enough, losing it can feel like losing something that already existed.

That is one reason limerent disappointment can be confusingly intense. Other people may look at the situation and think: *But you weren’t even together.* Your nervous system does not necessarily care about that distinction in the moment. You may be grieving hundreds of imagined mornings. A home you never shared. Conversations you never had. Sex that never happened. Trips never booked. A version of yourself that never had the chance to exist.

The grief is real even when the future was imagined. What matters is learning to call both things by their correct names. *Real grief. Imagined future.* Those can exist together.

**Every signal becomes personal**

A person in limerence can become extremely sensitive to changes in contact. This does not always look dramatic from the outside. It can be a series of tiny internal movements.

Avery normally sends three-line replies. Today Avery sends “lol.” Riley notices immediately. Yesterday Avery was affectionate. Today Avery seems distracted. Last week Avery initiated two conversations. This week Riley initiated all of them.

None of those observations is invented. The shift may be real. The problem begins when observation and explanation become fused.

*Avery sent a shorter message* becomes *Avery is losing interest.*
*Avery spent more time talking with someone else* becomes *I am being replaced.*
*Avery was tired* becomes *Something has changed between us.*

Once that happens, the mind no longer experiences interpretation as interpretation. It experiences it as reality. This matters because our emotions respond not only to events but to the meaning we assign them. If you believe a delayed reply means someone is abandoning you, your body can react to abandonment even if the other person is asleep.

The fear is real. The explanation may still be wrong.

That distinction is one of the hardest and most useful skills in this entire book. You do not need to dismiss your feelings to question your interpretation. You can say: *My panic is real*, and also: *I do not yet know what this means.* Both statements can be true at the same time.

**The emotional slot machine problem**

You may have heard limerence compared with gambling or addiction. There is a useful observation hidden inside that comparison, but the language often becomes much more certain than the science allows.

Unpredictable rewards can be powerful teachers. When a behavior sometimes produces relief or reward and sometimes does not, the behavior may become surprisingly persistent. That principle is well established in learning research.

What we cannot honestly say is that psychologists have proven limerence is a “dopamine addiction” to another person. They have not. Romantic attraction involves brain systems related to reward and motivation, and there are meaningful similarities between craving states and aspects of passionate romantic love. But translating that into “you are addicted to dopamine from your crush” turns a complicated research area into a catchy slogan.

We will use a more careful model later. For now, the important part is behavioral. Riley feels uncertainty. Riley checks. Sometimes there is nothing. Sometimes there is a message from Avery. When there is, the relief is immediate.

That relief matters. It teaches Riley that checking is worth doing. The next time uncertainty appears, the urge arrives faster. This is how ordinary behavior can gradually become a loop. Not because Riley is weak. Because the behavior worked. For thirty seconds. The problem is what happens after the thirty seconds are over.

**When hope becomes regulation**

There is one more piece we need before we can understand why this experience becomes so powerful.

Avery does not only produce romantic excitement. Avery changes how Riley feels about Riley.

On days when Avery is attentive, Riley feels funny. Attractive. Interesting. Alive. The workday seems easier. Music sounds better in the car. The future feels open.

On days when Avery seems distant, Riley becomes restless and self-critical. Nothing else has objectively changed. But something important has happened internally. Another person’s attention has become a form of emotional regulation.

Emotional regulation means the ways we influence, manage, tolerate, or respond to our emotional states. We all use other people for regulation. A child calms in a safe parent’s arms. Adults call friends when they are upset. Partners soothe each other after difficult days. Healthy relationships are full of co-regulation, which simply means that human beings help one another return to steadier emotional states.

The problem is not needing people. The problem begins when one particular person’s behavior gains an extreme amount of control over whether you feel okay.

That is a heavy job for any relationship. It is an especially unstable job when the relationship itself is uncertain. If Avery’s attention now regulates Riley’s sense of hope, worth, and aliveness, then every change in Avery’s behavior becomes more than social information. It becomes emotional weather.

That is one reason a delayed reply can feel much bigger than the delay itself. The mind is not simply asking: *Will Avery text me back?* It may also be asking: *Am I still wanted? Am I still special? Is the future still possible? Do I still get to feel like that version of myself?*

Those are much larger questions. And if you are trying to answer them through one person’s text messages, you are going to need a lot of text messages.

**Three things to begin noticing**

We are not going to end every chapter with homework. Understanding deserves room to breathe. But there are three distinctions worth carrying with you from here.

When you find yourself thinking about the person, notice which layer you are in:
1. **The person:** Who are they, based on what you actually know?
2. **The possibility:** What future are you imagining with them?
3. **The regulation:** What happens inside you when they seem closer or farther away?

You may discover that these overlap. That is expected. The point is not to force them apart. The point is to start seeing them. Because the more they blur together, the easier it becomes to believe that wanting the person, wanting the future, and needing emotional relief are all the same need.

They are not always the same. That matters because only one of them may truly require Avery.

### **Chapter 2: What This Is—and What It Isn’t**

Three people can say the same sentence and mean three very different things.

“I can’t stop thinking about them.”

For one person, it means: *I met someone six weeks ago, I’m falling in love, and everything feels new.*
For another: *I love my partner, but I keep having intrusive doubts about whether I really love them enough. I check my feelings constantly because I need to know for sure.*
For someone else: *They are the first person I think about when I wake up and the last person I think about before I sleep. I study every interaction for evidence they might want me back. When they seem close, I feel almost euphoric. When they pull away, I can barely focus.*

On the surface, all three people are preoccupied with romance. Underneath, different processes may be at work. That distinction matters because psychological experiences cannot be identified by one behavior alone.

Checking a phone can mean excitement. Fear. Habit. OCD. Loneliness. Avoidance. Jealousy. Or nothing particularly important. The useful question is not simply: *What are you doing?* It is: *What function is the behavior serving, and what happens if you cannot do it?*

This chapter is not going to give you a flowchart that ends with a diagnosis. That would promise more certainty than the evidence can support. Instead, we are going to look at several experiences that can resemble limerence and ask a more careful question: *What seems to be driving the loop?*

**Intense attraction is not automatically limerence**

Early romantic attraction can be wonderfully unreasonable. People think about one another constantly. They lose sleep talking. They reread messages because the messages make them happy. They daydream. They idealize. They want to be close. They feel a rush when affection is returned.

That creates an immediate problem for anyone trying to define limerence as “thinking about someone all the time.” People in love do that too.

A better distinction is flexibility and cost. Imagine Elena. She has been dating Sam for two months. She thinks about Sam while driving home. She gets excited when Sam’s name appears on her phone. She tells her sister far too many details about their dates.

Sam sometimes takes hours to respond. Elena notices. She occasionally wonders whether Sam is losing interest. Then she finishes dinner, walks the dog, answers work emails, calls a friend, and returns to the rest of her life.

Her attraction is intense. But it has not become the organizing principle of her emotional world. She does not need to repeatedly inspect the relationship to keep herself steady. She can tolerate some uncertainty without trying to solve it all night.

Contrast that with Riley. Riley’s attraction to Avery is beginning to affect sleep, attention, mood, the relationship with Morgan, and the ability to leave questions unanswered. The amount of feeling is not the only difference. The relationship between feeling and functioning has changed.

That is why it can be more useful to ask: *How much room does this feeling leave for the rest of your life?* rather than: *How intense is it?*

**The Spectrum of Romantic Preoccupation**

To help clarify where your experience might sit, consider this sliding scale. It is not a diagnostic tool, but a visual way to understand the transition from normal passion to impairing fixation.

`[ Ordinary Passion ] <-----> [ Infatuation / Crush ] <-----> [ Limerence ] <-----> [ Clinical OCD (ROCD) ]`

*   **Ordinary Passion:** High excitement, some distraction, but attention easily returns to daily life. Flexibility remains high.
*   **Infatuation / Crush:** Intense idealization, frequent thoughts, but does not severely destabilize self-worth or disrupt core responsibilities.
*   **Limerence:** Involuntary, intrusive preoccupation. Emotional stability depends heavily on signs of reciprocation. Marked difficulty tolerating uncertainty. Noticeable neglect of self or other responsibilities.
*   **Clinical OCD (ROCD):** Ego-dystonic (unwanted) intrusive doubts about the relationship or partner. Distress is not about hoped-for reciprocation, but about resolving terrifying uncertainty regarding "rightness." Requires clinical diagnosis.

If your experience involves recurring preoccupation, dependence on signs of reciprocation, difficulty tolerating uncertainty, increasing fantasy or checking, and meaningful interference with the rest of your life, then the tools in this book may be useful even if your experience does not fit every research definition perfectly.

**Unrequited love is not always limerence**

Loving someone who does not love you back can be devastating. There is no need to make the grief pathological before we take it seriously. You may know the relationship is impossible and still miss the person every day. You may cry. You may dream about them. You may need months to stop expecting their name to appear on your phone. That can be heartbreak.

Limerence can overlap with unrequited love, but there is often a different mental quality when the attachment becomes dominated by unresolved possibility and repeated interpretation.

Imagine two people after rejection.
The first thinks: *I wish they had chosen me. I still love them. I know they don’t want this, and I hate that I have to let go.*
The second thinks: *They said they can’t be with me right now, but what exactly does “right now” mean? They cried when they said it. If they felt nothing, why would they cry? Maybe this is about timing. Maybe they need space. Maybe if I stay present without pushing—*

Both people are grieving. The second person is also keeping possibility alive through interpretation. That does not make them foolish. Hope is difficult to put down when reality leaves even a small opening—or when the mind can create one.

One of the hardest parts of recovery can be learning that grief often requires an answer that hope keeps appealing. You do not need to stop loving someone before you accept what is available. Those are separate processes.

**Anxious attachment is broader than one person**

Attachment theory is one of the most common explanations people encounter when researching limerence. Attachment anxiety describes a pattern in which closeness can feel uncertain and rejection or abandonment carries a great deal of emotional threat.

Recent limerence research does support a connection, but not a simple one.

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> In the 2025 LQ-11 study, attachment anxiety was associated with the scale’s intense-attachment dimension, but it was *not* significantly related to the neglect dimension. Avoidant attachment was not significantly associated with either factor. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*
> The 2026 research also found elevated insecure attachment among participants reporting impairing limerence. *(Evans et al., 2026)*

That makes attachment relevant. It does not make attachment the whole explanation.

One helpful distinction is scope. Anxious attachment often appears as a broader relational pattern. You may worry across close relationships that people will leave. Limerence, by contrast, often creates extraordinary concentration around a particular person and the possibility of reciprocation.

The two can absolutely overlap. For Riley, perhaps they do. Riley may already carry a deep sensitivity to being left and then encounter Avery at exactly the right moment for that sensitivity to attach itself to a new possibility. But someone else may have relatively secure long-term relationships and still become limerent during an unusual period of loneliness, change, novelty, or emotional deprivation.

You are not a type. You are a person with patterns that may become more or less active in different relationships and different seasons of life.

**Relationship OCD: when the question itself becomes the trap**

Relationship obsessive-compulsive disorder (ROCD) is not simply “overthinking your relationship.” It refers to obsessive-compulsive symptoms centered on a romantic relationship or partner. Obsessions are recurring intrusive thoughts, doubts, images, or urges that create significant distress. Compulsions are behaviors or mental acts used to reduce that distress or gain certainty.

A person with relationship-centered ROCD might repeatedly wonder: *Do I really love my partner? Are they the right person? What if I’m making a terrible mistake by staying? What if I don’t feel enough?*

There is obvious overlap with limerence. Both can involve repetitive thinking, checking, reassurance seeking, intolerance of uncertainty, and a desperate desire to know. But the central questions often differ.

In ROCD, the mind may demand certainty about the relationship itself: *Is this right? Do I love them enough?*
In limerence, the mind is often pulled toward reciprocation and possibility: *Do they want me? Could this happen? What did that interaction mean?*

A shorthand I sometimes use is: **ROCD can become a doubt loop. Limerence can become a hope loop.** That is a useful memory aid. It is not a diagnostic law. Real OCD can contain hope. Limerence contains plenty of doubt. And someone can experience both.

If obsessive thoughts and compulsions are consuming large amounts of time or causing significant distress, an OCD-informed clinician can help determine what is actually happening. Mislabeling OCD as limerence can delay treatment that has a much stronger evidence base.

**The “Favorite Person” idea**

If you spend time in mental health communities online, especially communities centered on borderline personality disorder, you may have heard the term *Favorite Person* (FP). It often describes a relationship in which one person becomes unusually central to emotional stability, identity, reassurance, or fear of abandonment. That can sound very similar to limerence.

The important thing to know is that “Favorite Person” is community language, not a formal diagnosis. Nor should every intense dependency be collapsed into limerence.

One potentially useful distinction is the emotional job the relationship is performing. In a Favorite Person-type dynamic, the relationship may function primarily as an attachment and regulation system: *I need you close so I feel safe.* Limerence may be organized more strongly around hoped-for reciprocation: *I need to know that you might choose me.*

Real human beings refuse to stay inside tidy boxes. Someone may experience both. The point is not to win a naming contest. The point is to understand the mechanism well enough to respond to it.

**Autistic intensity should not be mistaken for pathology**

Autistic people can have deep, focused interests. These interests may be joyful, regulating, absorbing, meaningful, and central to identity. Sometimes the subject of intense interest can involve another person: a public figure, character, artist, historical person, friend, mentor, or someone fascinating to know. Online, people may describe this with terms such as “person hyperfixation” or “human special interest.”

Thinking about somebody frequently does not automatically make the interest unhealthy. Learning everything about someone does not automatically mean you want romantic reciprocation.

A more useful question is: *What is the focus doing?*
Does thinking about this person bring curiosity, pleasure, inspiration, structure, or connection? Or has the focus become increasingly dependent on whether they respond, approve, reciprocate, remain available, or choose you?

Can you enjoy the interest while respecting their separateness? Can they disappoint you without destabilizing your identity? If yes, intensity itself may not be the problem. If no, another layer may be present. A person can be autistic and limerent. But we should never use limerence as a convenient label for neurodivergent intensity simply because neurotypical observers find that intensity unusual.

**What about ADHD and hyperfocus?**

You may have seen claims like: *“People with ADHD are more prone to limerence because of dopamine.”* At the moment, direct research does not justify those statements. ADHD can involve attention that is difficult to regulate, strong responses to novelty, and periods of intense focus. Those features make the proposed connection understandable. Understandable is not the same thing as established.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> A recent scoping review examining limerence and neighboring constructs identified the lack of direct peer-reviewed evidence connecting ADHD and limerence as a significant gap in the literature. *(Springer, 2024)*

If you have ADHD and recognize limerence in yourself, your ADHD may affect how the experience feels or how difficult it is to redirect attention. But we should not tell a scientific story we do not yet have evidence to tell.

**Grief can look obsessive without being limerence**

After rejection or breakup, the mind often keeps returning to the lost person. Attachment does not disappear on the same day a relationship ends. You may instinctively look for them in places they used to be. You may replay the final conversation. You may check their social media even though you know it hurts. None of this automatically means limerence.

Grief says: *I hate that this is over.*
Limerent rumination may say: *But is it really over?*

Again, people can experience both. Human psychology is full of overlapping processes. That is why mechanism matters more than labels.

**A better way to ask the question**

By now, you may feel less certain about whether your experience “counts” as limerence than when the chapter started. That is not a failure. It may mean we are asking a better question.

Instead of: *What label proves what I am experiencing?*
try: *What process keeps this experience going?*

Consider several dimensions:
*   **What is the central concern?** Are you trying to determine whether they reciprocate? Whether your existing relationship is right? Whether you can tolerate rejection?
*   **What role does uncertainty play?** Does uncertainty simply annoy you? Or does it keep the entire attachment mentally active?
*   **What happens when you check?** Do you gain useful information? Or do you experience a few minutes of relief followed by another question?
*   **How much choice remains?** Can you redirect attention? Can you tolerate not knowing? Can you respect a boundary even when it hurts?
*   **What is happening to the rest of your life?** Has the focus displaced sleep? Work? Parenting? Friendship? A partner? Creativity? Appetite?

These questions tell us more than whether you score enough points to earn a label. They tell us where suffering is occurring. And, just as importantly, where change may be possible.

The next chapter moves away from categories almost entirely. Because once your mind has become occupied by another person, knowing what to call the experience does not explain what it is like to live inside it. We need to go there next.

### **Chapter 3: Life Inside the Limerent Mind**

Riley is supposed to be working. The document on the screen has been open for forty minutes. Three sentences have been written.

Avery is not in the room. There is no conversation happening. No message waiting. No new information. Yet Avery has been present almost the entire time.

First Riley remembers yesterday’s conversation. Then rewrites part of it. *I should have said…* Then imagines what Avery might have thought afterward. Then imagines telling Avery something personal next time they are alone. Then imagines Avery responding perfectly.

That becomes another scene. The scene becomes a dinner. The dinner becomes a confession. The confession becomes a relationship.

Then Riley notices the clock. Seven minutes have disappeared.

Back to work. Thirty seconds later: *But what if Avery really did mean—*

This is one reason limerence can be exhausting even before anyone does anything. The relationship is not only happening between two people. A second version is happening internally, all day.

**When someone begins living in your attention**

Attention feels passive until you try to move it. Most of the time, you do not notice how easily your mind shifts from one thing to another. You read. You work. You cook. You notice traffic. You answer a question.

Limerence can change the texture of that process. The person becomes unusually *salient*. Salience simply means something stands out and captures attention. Your mind becomes good at finding them. Their name in a list. Their car model in traffic. A song they mentioned. A place you went together. A scent. A date. A color they wore. A notification sound. A mutual friend’s post.

You are not deliberately conducting a search every time. Sometimes the mind seems to retrieve them before you have chosen anything. That can be deeply frustrating.

People often respond by giving themselves instructions: *Stop thinking about them. This is ridiculous. Think about literally anything else.* Then, five minutes later, there they are again. The failure can feel personal. *If I were stronger, I could control my own mind.*

But thoughts do not work that way. You cannot fully control which thought appears next. Trying to forcibly suppress a thought can sometimes increase how noticeable that thought becomes. That is one reason recovery cannot depend on achieving perfect control over mental content.

The better question is not: *How do I make sure Avery never appears in my mind?*
It is: *What happens after Avery appears?*

That is where we begin to find choice.

**Thought, elaboration, behavior**

Imagine Riley walking through a grocery store. A song starts playing. Avery once said they loved this song.

A thought appears: *Avery.*
That first moment may be automatic. Riley did not choose the song. Did not choose the memory. Did not deliberately summon the person.

Now comes a second moment. Riley imagines sending Avery the song. Then imagines Avery answering. Then imagines the conversation becoming playful. Then remembers the time Avery touched Riley’s arm. Then replays the touch. Then starts wondering whether it lasted longer than a normal touch. Then imagines what would happen if they were alone together.

Five minutes later, Riley is still standing in the produce aisle and has barely noticed the groceries.

That is *elaboration*. Elaboration means taking a thought and building upon it.

This is an important distinction because people often collapse the entire chain into: *I can’t control thinking about them.* The first thought may indeed be difficult to control. The next five minutes contain more moving parts.

Then there is behavior. Riley takes out the phone. Opens Avery’s social profile. Checks whether Avery has posted. Looks at a photo. Now the internal loop has become an external action.

So we have three different events:
1. **Thought** (Automatic)
2. **Elaboration** (Mental engagement)
3. **Behavior** (Physical action)

They can happen so quickly that they feel like one thing. They are not one thing. This distinction is not a way to blame yourself for every fantasy. It is a way to stop treating the entire experience as untouchable. You may not control which memories appear. You can gradually develop more choice about how long you rehearse them. You may not control the first urge to check. You can learn to place time between urge and action.

Those are small differences. Small differences become important when repeated hundreds of times.

**Fantasy is doing something for you**

It is tempting to treat fantasy as the enemy. That usually makes the conversation too simple. Fantasy exists because imagination is useful. We rehearse possible conversations. We plan futures. We explore desire. We soothe ourselves with memories. We create stories.

Romantic fantasy can be pleasurable, creative, erotic, and completely harmless. So the question is not: *Do you fantasize about them?* The better question is: *What does the fantasy do for you?*

*   **Sometimes it offers excitement.** Real life may feel painfully repetitive. Work. Bills. Chores. Caregiving. Same room. Same conversations. Then Avery appears. Suddenly the future has color again. Fantasy becomes a source of novelty.
*   **Sometimes it offers companionship.** If you feel lonely, the imagined person may be more available than the real one. You can talk to them internally whenever you want. They understand the story because you wrote both sides.
*   **Sometimes fantasy offers control.** Real relationships contain other people’s freedom. They misunderstand you. Say no. Get tired. Want things you do not want. Fantasy does not have to. The other person can respond exactly the way you need.
*   **Sometimes fantasy offers self-esteem.** You imagine the moment Avery finally chooses you. And inside that scene, you become someone worth choosing.
*   **Sometimes it offers escape.** You do not have to think about your marriage. Your job. Your loneliness. Your body. Your age. Your grief. Your fear that life has become smaller than you hoped. For a little while, there is only possibility.

None of this means you consciously invented limerence because you wanted a coping strategy. That is not how coping usually works. Human beings repeat what produces relief. Often long before they understand why.

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> In the 2025 LQ-11 validation study, higher limerence scores were associated with greater imaginative fantasy and psychological inflexibility, which is the tendency to become rigidly caught in responses to difficult internal experiences rather than flexibly acting according to one’s values. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*

That does not prove fantasy causes limerence. It suggests fantasy is part of the terrain worth understanding.

**When escape becomes residence**

A fantasy can begin as a place you visit. Then become a place you live. That change is subtle.

At first, Riley imagines Avery because it feels good. Later, Riley imagines Avery because ordinary life without that inner relationship feels flat.

This is where the emotional economics change. The fantasy is no longer only adding pleasure. It is helping manage the absence of pleasure elsewhere. Think about the difference. You can enjoy dessert after dinner. Or you can discover that dessert is the only thing that makes eating feel worth doing. The dessert itself has not changed. Its psychological job has.

The same thing can happen with romantic fantasy. If thinking about Avery is one source of joy among many, there is flexibility. If thinking about Avery becomes the most reliable source of excitement, comfort, hope, erotic energy, validation, and escape available to Riley, then asking Riley to “stop fantasizing” is not a small request. It feels like asking Riley to give up the one place where life still feels alive.

This is why behavior change can initially feel worse. The fantasy was doing work. If you remove a coping behavior without understanding its job, the need does not vanish. You simply become more aware of what the coping behavior was covering. Later in this book, we will spend a great deal of time on that problem. For now, it is enough to recognize that an internal relationship can become emotionally useful even while it is also hurting you. Both can be true.

**The conversation that never happened**

One of the most common forms of limerent fantasy is conversation rehearsal. You imagine what you will say. What they will say. How you will explain yourself. How they will finally understand. Sometimes the conversation is romantic. Sometimes confrontational. Sometimes it is a perfect accident: *you happen to be alone together; the subject comes up naturally; you are unusually articulate; they admit they have felt the same thing all along.*

These imagined conversations can feel productive because language is involved. You are thinking. Preparing. Processing. But there is an important difference between useful rehearsal and repetitive simulation.

Useful rehearsal eventually helps you do something. Repetitive simulation often keeps replacing action. You imagine telling Avery how you feel instead of deciding whether telling Avery would be appropriate. You imagine setting a boundary instead of setting one. You imagine Morgan understanding everything perfectly instead of having the difficult conversation that might not go perfectly.

Fantasy can give you the emotional experience of movement while your actual life remains still. That is one reason months can pass. Internally, the relationship is constantly developing. Externally, very little changes. The mind mistakes activity for progress.

**Replaying the past**

The future is not the only place limerence lives. It also edits the past. A memory occurs. Avery hugged you after a difficult day. At the time, it felt kind. Three weeks later, after your feelings have grown, the memory changes emotional color.

*Was that hug actually different?* You replay it. *How long did it last? Where were Avery’s hands? Did Avery pull away first? What happened immediately afterward?*

Memory feels like stored video. It is not. Human memory is reconstructive. We do not retrieve perfect recordings. We rebuild experiences using pieces of stored information, present emotion, later knowledge, and expectation. That does not mean the hug never happened. It means repeated analysis does not necessarily bring you closer to certainty. Sometimes it increases confidence without increasing accuracy.

This becomes especially important when you are trying to infer another person’s private state. You can remember Avery touching your arm. You cannot remember Avery’s intention unless Avery told you. That intention exists outside your memory. The mind may fill it in anyway.

*They wanted an excuse to touch me.* Possible.
*They were being affectionate because they care about me.* Possible.
*They touch everyone like that.* Possible.
*They did not think about it at all.* Also possible.

The emotional brain dislikes that list. It wants the answer. Fantasy supplies one. Fear supplies another. Neither becomes evidence simply because it feels convincing. We will build a much more detailed tool for separating observation from interpretation later in the book. For now, I want you to become suspicious of one particular sentence: *“I just know.”*

Sometimes you do know. Sometimes *I just know* is what certainty feels like when evidence has run out.

**Your phone is a portal**

Dorothy Tennov first wrote about limerence in a world without read receipts. Without typing bubbles. Without active-status dots. Without Instagram stories. Without location sharing. Without the ability to search years of somebody’s photos from bed at two in the morning.

That matters. Modern technology did not invent romantic obsession. It did make information about other people extraordinarily available. Not complete information. Fragments. And fragments are excellent material for an uncertain mind.

Avery has not texted Riley, but Avery posted a story. *What does that mean?*
Avery watched Riley’s story but did not respond to the message. *What does that mean?*
Avery liked an old photo. *Accident? Signal?*
Avery followed someone new. *Who is that?*

Digital platforms allow a person to maintain a sense of contact without actual contact. You can know that Avery is awake. Know what restaurant Avery visited. See who commented. Know that Avery has seen something you posted. Know that Avery was online nine minutes ago. And still know absolutely nothing about how Avery feels about you.

That combination—high access, low certainty—can be brutal. Research specifically demonstrating social media’s causal role in limerence remains limited, and recent reviews identify digital environments as an important area for further study rather than a settled explanation. That uncertainty should not stop us from recognizing the behavioral reality.

Twenty years ago, many forms of checking required effort. Now they require a thumb. Low friction changes behavior. If the urge can be satisfied instantly, the mind has fewer natural opportunities to discover that an urge can rise and fall without action. That becomes important in recovery.

**Checking rarely gives the certainty it promises**

Riley checks Avery’s profile. Nothing new. For a moment, that feels like information.
Then another thought appears. *Maybe Avery hasn’t been online.*
Riley checks active status. Offline. Relief.
Then: *Or maybe Avery turned it off.*
Another search becomes possible.

This is how checking can become self-defeating. The behavior promises closure. Instead, it often creates another branch of the question.

You may know this feeling. You check whether they viewed your story. They did. Relief. Then: *But they didn’t react to it.* You see they liked your post. Relief. Then: *They like everyone’s posts.* You receive a warm message. Relief. Then: *Was it friendly or romantic?* You receive a clearly romantic message. Relief. Then: *Do they mean it as much as I do?*

The location of uncertainty changes. Uncertainty itself survives. This is one of the reasons reassurance can become bottomless. No answer can permanently solve a question that the mind keeps learning how to rewrite.

That does not mean you should ignore real information. It means we need to distinguish information-seeking from anxiety-relief behavior. If Avery tells Riley, “I only want friendship,” that is information. If Riley spends three hours reviewing every affectionate interaction to determine whether Avery secretly means something else, the behavior is no longer clarifying reality. It is negotiating with it.

**Thought suppression and the rebound trap**

When people become frightened by their own preoccupation, they often become more aggressive toward their thoughts. *Stop. Enough. Why am I still thinking about this? I should be over it.*

That inner fight can become another form of attention. Now you are thinking about Avery and monitoring whether you are thinking about Avery. Every recurrence becomes evidence that you are failing.

This is one reason we will eventually use approaches that emphasize a different relationship with thought. Not agreement. Not indulgence. Not suppression. Recognition.

*Avery thought. Fantasy starting. Urge to check.*

That may sound almost too simple. It is not easy. There is a large difference between: *Avery is meant for me.* and: *I am having the thought that Avery is meant for me.*

The second sentence creates a little space between the thought and reality. Psychologists working in acceptance-based therapies sometimes call this *cognitive defusion*. Defusion means learning to experience thoughts as mental events rather than automatically treating them as commands, facts, or accurate descriptions of reality. We will return to that much later with practical methods.

At this stage, the concept matters because it offers an alternative to two extremes. You do not have to believe every thought. You do not have to fight every thought. You can notice it. That is the beginning of flexibility.

**The choice point is not where you think**

People often imagine recovery beginning with a dramatic decision: *I am done.* Sometimes that happens. A boundary is crossed. A rejection becomes undeniable. A partner discovers the relationship. The cost becomes too high. The person blocks contact and makes a clean break.

But many forms of change happen in much smaller moments.
Riley notices the urge to open Avery’s profile. That is a moment.
Riley notices a fantasy starting while driving. That is a moment.
Riley begins drafting a message whose real purpose is not communication but reassurance. That is a moment.
Riley starts turning Avery’s silence into a story about being unwanted. Another moment.

None feels life-changing. Together they are the life of the pattern. This is good news. It means you do not have to solve the entire attachment every time you become activated. You need to learn where the next choice exists.

Sometimes the choice will be behavioral. *Do I check? Do I message? Do I engineer an encounter?*
Sometimes it will be attentional. *Do I continue this fantasy for the next twenty minutes?*
Sometimes it will be interpretive. *Do I know what this means?*
Sometimes it will be relational. *Can I respect a boundary without punishing the other person for setting it?*

Automatic does not mean inevitable. The beginning of an internal event may be involuntary. The entire chain does not have to remain so.

**The life being displaced**

There is another cost of preoccupation that is easy to miss because it does not always hurt. *Opportunity cost*.

An opportunity cost is what you lose by spending a limited resource in one place instead of another. Attention is limited. Time is limited. Imagination is limited. Emotional energy is limited.

Riley spends forty minutes thinking about Avery. Those forty minutes do not vanish from nowhere. They come out of something else. Work. Sleep. A conversation with Morgan. Reading. Exercise. Calling a parent. Playing with the dog. Doing absolutely nothing.

The same is true of emotional anticipation. If the most exciting part of Riley’s day is waiting for Avery to text, ordinary pleasures may start feeling weaker by comparison. That does not prove ordinary life is empty. It may mean attention has been trained toward one especially intense source of reward.

Over time, this can create a painful illusion: *Avery is the only person who makes me feel alive.*
Perhaps. Or perhaps Avery has become the place where more and more of Riley’s capacity for aliveness is being spent.

Those possibilities lead to very different futures. One says: *I need Avery.* The other asks: *What has happened to the rest of my life?*

We will not answer that too quickly. Sometimes the answer really is that something important has gone missing. A marriage can become emotionally empty. A job can become soul-crushing. People do sometimes meet someone who reveals truths they have been avoiding. Limerence should never become a tool for dismissing those truths.

If Riley realizes, through knowing Avery, that the relationship with Morgan has serious problems, the appropriate conclusion is not: *Those problems are imaginary because I’m limerent.* The appropriate conclusion is: *I need to evaluate my relationship with Morgan based on the reality of my relationship with Morgan.* Avery should not have to serve as the proof.

A fantasy can reveal a need. It cannot automatically tell you how that need should be met.

**Before we ask why**

At this point, you may already have explanations for yourself. Maybe you know you were lonely. Maybe your childhood was unstable. Maybe rejection has always terrified you. Maybe you have ADHD and recognize how intensely your attention can lock onto something compelling. Maybe you have been unhappy in your relationship for years.

Do not force an origin story yet. We will get there. A premature explanation can become another kind of certainty-seeking. *This happened because of my mother. This happened because of dopamine. This happened because Avery is my soulmate.* Different stories. Same temptation. The wish to make complexity resolve into one clean answer.

For now, stay closer to what we can observe.
A thought appears. Sometimes you elaborate it. Sometimes you act. The action may provide relief. The fantasy may provide something your current life is not providing enough of. Your attention keeps returning. The person gradually becomes connected not only with desire, but with possibility, regulation, and identity.

That is already enough to explain why telling yourself to “just stop” has not worked. It also gives us somewhere better to go.

The next part of this book asks a deeper question: *What hunger has gathered around this person?* Not because every limerent attachment comes from a wound. Not because wanting someone must secretly mean wanting something else. But because another person can become far more powerful in our inner world when they begin carrying needs that extend beyond romance.

Security. Recognition. Belonging. Aliveness. Escape. Hope. The desire to become someone new. Before we can loosen the attachment, we need to understand what it has been asked to hold.

### **Part II: Why the Mind Gets Stuck**

---

### **Chapter 4: The Hunger Beneath the Person**

When Riley tries to explain what is so compelling about Avery, the answers come easily.

Avery is funny. Curious. Warm without being fake. The kind of person who notices things. Avery asks follow-up questions instead of waiting for a turn to speak. Remembers details. Gets excited about strange little subjects. Has a way of making an ordinary conversation feel like the most interesting thing happening in the room.

All of that may be true. 

Then Riley says something different. 
“I feel more like myself around Avery.”

That sentence deserves attention. At first, it sounds like another compliment. Maybe it is. But notice where the subject of the sentence changed. Riley is no longer describing Avery. Riley is describing Riley.

Around Avery, Riley is quicker. Funnier. More spontaneous. Less tired. Less responsible for everybody. More interesting. More sexual. More willing to say what comes to mind. Around Avery, there seems to be another version of life nearby.

That does not make the attraction fake. It makes it larger. 

Sometimes we are drawn toward another person partly because of who they are. Sometimes we are also drawn toward who we become in their presence. And sometimes those two experiences become so tightly intertwined that losing the person starts to feel like losing access to that version of ourselves.

This is where the question of limerence becomes more complicated. If Avery has started representing not just affection but aliveness, recognition, freedom, hope, possibility, and proof that Riley still has a self outside the routines of ordinary life, then Avery is carrying much more psychological weight than one relationship can reasonably hold.

Understanding that weight does not mean explaining the person away. It means finally asking what the attachment has been asked to do.

**Wanting people is not a pathology**

Before we talk about unmet needs, we need to be careful with the phrase itself. “Unmet need” can sound like therapy language for something defective. It can also create the impression that emotionally healthy people should somehow graduate from needing other human beings. They do not.

Humans are intensely social creatures. Connection is tied to mental and physical well-being across a very large research literature. 

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Loneliness refers not simply to being physically alone but to the painful sense that the connection available to you is not meeting your social needs. Perceived social disconnection can increase vigilance toward social threat and motivate attempts to reconnect. *(PubMed Central)*

We need people. We want to be known. We want to matter somewhere. None of those desires proves that something went wrong in childhood. The mistake is not needing people. The problem develops when one person becomes responsible for satisfying such a large concentration of need that their availability starts deciding whether life feels meaningful.

The person can become a symbol. A symbol is something that carries meaning beyond itself. A wedding ring is metal; it is also commitment. A childhood home is a building; it may also mean safety, grief, or everything you escaped. 

Riley has been with Morgan for eleven years. That history contains real love. It also contains bills, illness, arguments, compromises, repetitive weekends, work stress, family obligations, repairs, groceries, disappointments, affection, loyalty, and hundreds of quiet days that were never designed to be exciting. Avery enters without carrying any of that. Avery arrives associated with conversation, humor, interest, possibility. The lightness of not having fought about the dishwasher six hundred times.

That difference can become psychologically powerful. Riley may conclude: *I feel alive with Avery because Avery is the person who makes me alive.* 

Maybe. But another possibility is that Avery has become associated with a part of Riley that has been underused. Curiosity. Novelty. Risk. Erotic energy. Play. The wish to be known as something other than employee, parent, partner, caretaker, reliable one.

If Avery contains Riley’s aliveness, there is only one solution: get Avery. If Avery reveals Riley’s hunger for aliveness, many more possibilities become visible. 

> 💡 **Micro-Observation Prompt:**
> *Take ten seconds to ask yourself: "When they seem close to me, I get to feel ______." Not what do you get from them. What do you get to feel?*

**Being seen can feel like being given back to yourself**

There is a particular kind of encounter that can become very powerful for people who have felt unnoticed for a long time. Someone pays specific attention. They notice your humor. Ask about something you care about. Remember what overwhelms you. 

Maybe you have spent years feeling like the useful one rather than the interesting one. Then somebody looks directly at a part of you that has gone unseen. That can feel enormous. *Finally. Finally someone understands. Finally I am visible.*

The longing that follows may seem to be entirely about the person. Sometimes it is also about not wanting to lose the mirror. A mirror, in psychological language, does not mean someone who flatters you constantly. It means another person’s response helps you experience and understand something about yourself.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Research has found that self-esteem can become responsive to important close relationships, including romantic bonds. The danger comes when your reflection becomes available in only one mirror. Then any movement away from you can feel like disappearance. *(PubMed Central)*

Recent limerence research gives us one direct piece of evidence here. 

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> In the 2025 study developing the Limerence Questionnaire, greater limerence was associated with a stronger need to belong—the desire to form and maintain meaningful social bonds. The same study also found associations with lower self-esteem and greater imaginative fantasy. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*

Someone might hear “need to belong” and think: *There it is. Limerent people are just needy.* That would badly misunderstand both the research and ordinary human life. Belonging is a fundamental human concern. The interesting question is not whether you need it. It is where you have learned to look for it.

Loneliness is not the same as having nobody. You can be lonely in a marriage. Lonely in a family. You can be lonely in a group chat that never stops making noise. Research on loneliness emphasizes perceived social disconnection. 

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> In the 2026 experience-sampling study, limerent thinking appeared to vary with mood, particularly sadness and loneliness. The timing of the fantasy often tells you more than the content. *(Evans et al., 2026)*

*Riley puts the phone down and walks to the window, letting the cool air settle the thoughts before returning to the screen.*

Sometimes the hunger is expansion. The self-expansion model proposes that relationships can enlarge our sense of who we are by exposing us to new perspectives, experiences, skills, identities, and possibilities. New relationships can be especially powerful because there is so much to discover. 

If your ordinary life is crowded with obligation, another person may represent escape from being needed. Avery does not ask Riley to remember the dentist appointment. Around Avery, Riley gets to arrive as an individual rather than as one half of a household system. That can feel like freedom.

**When being chosen becomes proof**

There is another need that often hides inside romantic longing. Worth. Not abstract self-esteem. Something more specific. The fantasy that another person’s choice can settle an argument you have been having with yourself. *If Avery chooses Riley, perhaps that proves Riley is still attractive. Still interesting. Still capable of inspiring desire.*

This can be particularly powerful when the desired person seems difficult to obtain. A person whose attention is not easily won can become a more compelling source of proof precisely because their attention feels scarce. *Am I enough to be chosen?*

If the deeper problem is unstable worth, one person’s affection cannot permanently solve it. Because proof that must be repeatedly renewed never becomes proof.

**Childhood matters, but it is not the whole story**

This is where many books would give you a clean origin story. *Perhaps you had an inconsistent caregiver. Therefore, you became limerent.* 

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> The 2026 study of people reporting impairing limerence found elevated adverse childhood experiences and insecure attachment among participants. That is meaningful evidence. It is not a universal biography. *(Evans et al., 2026)*

Development is rarely that tidy. Life keeps happening after childhood. A secure person can become lonely. A satisfying marriage can deteriorate. A parent can disappear into caregiving for ten years and barely notice until someone else asks what they want. A person can move to a new city, experience bereavement, illness, divorce, or a sudden realization that the future now contains fewer years than the past. Then another person appears. Timing can become part of the chemistry.

Sometimes the current life is actually the problem. If you are obsessed with Avery, perhaps the answer must be inside your wounds. Not necessarily. Sometimes life is genuinely unsatisfying. Sometimes the relationship is full of resentment. 

If Avery reveals that Riley is starving for conversation, Riley should take that information seriously. But there is a crucial difference between: *This feeling is telling me something about my life*, and: *This feeling is telling me Avery is the solution.* Limerence can sometimes function like a flare. It lights up something in the dark. The mistake is assuming the flare is the destination.

### **Chapter 5: The Hope Loop**

At 10:14 Wednesday morning, Riley decides not to check. 

This is a real decision. Riley has noticed how ridiculous the pattern feels. The phone is beside the keyboard. Ten minutes pass. Then twelve. 

At thirteen minutes: *I wonder if Avery is in that meeting.*
At fourteen: *Maybe the message came through silently.*
At fifteen, Riley taps the screen. Nothing. There is a flash of disappointment. Then an odd sort of relief. *At least nothing has happened.* Riley puts the phone down.

Three minutes later, there is another possibility. *Maybe Avery posted something.* Instagram opens almost before the thought has finished. No post. But Avery was active seventeen minutes ago. 

Now the question has changed. *If Avery had time to be online, why not answer?* Riley exits the app. The chest feels tight. This was supposed to provide information. Instead, the mind now has a more interesting problem.

**The Hope Loop**

We can now name the pattern. I call it the Hope Loop. It is not a clinical diagnosis. It is a framework for organizing several processes we know something about: uncertainty, attention, interpretation, fantasy, urges, checking, temporary relief, and renewed uncertainty.

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: THE HOPE LOOP**
> `[Uncertainty] → [Salience] → [Interpretation] → [Fantasy] → [Urge] → [Checking/Contact] → [Relief] → [Renewed Uncertainty]`
> 
> *The loop can happen in five minutes. Or across several days. Once it becomes familiar, it can feel like one continuous emotional state. It is not. It has parts. Anything with parts creates more than one possible place for change.*

The human mind is drawn toward unfinished things. In romance, this can become especially powerful because the question is emotionally loaded. *Do they like me?* is rarely only a factual question. Depending on what has gathered around the person, the answer may seem to determine whether a future exists; whether you are desirable; whether you will be alone.

That is a lot of weight to place on a reply. 

When reality is uncertain, genuine ambiguity can still become an unhealthy organizing force. You do not need to prove the ambiguity is fake before asking whether living inside it is costing too much. Reality does not have to be simple for you to decide what you can live with.

Other times, the situation is less ambiguous than the person experiencing limerence wants it to be. Avery says: “I care about you, but I don’t want a romantic relationship.” That is meaningful information. Riley feels devastated. Then analysis begins. *Why say 'I care about you'? Why specify 'romantic'? Maybe Avery is afraid.* Now a clear limit has been converted into a new field of uncertainty. 

Hope itself is not bad. But hope becomes dangerous when it depends upon repeatedly editing another person’s *no* into a *maybe*. That does not honor hope. It denies autonomy.

**Checking is not always about information**

Imagine you hear a noise downstairs at two in the morning. You check the door. Locked. Good. That information solves the problem. You go back to bed. 

Now imagine checking the door. Locked. You return five minutes later. Still locked. Then again. At some point, the behavior is no longer primarily providing new information. It is providing relief.

When Riley checks Avery’s status, there may be some information. Online. Offline. Story posted. Message read. But the deeper function may be: *Make this feeling go away.* The behavior works. Briefly. 

Behavioral science has studied this kind of pattern in many contexts. When behavior reliably reduces an uncomfortable internal state, that relief can help reinforce the behavior. This is one reason compulsive patterns can seem irrational from the outside while feeling almost inevitable from within. The behavior is solving a problem. The problem is that the solution may help recreate the conditions that keep the problem alive.

Relief is not the same as resolution. Resolution changes what is known or accepted. Relief changes what you feel temporarily. If you repeatedly seek resolution through behaviors that mainly produce relief, you may remain trapped in the search.

**Why unpredictable rewards can become sticky**

You may have heard the phrase *intermittent reinforcement*. It means a behavior is rewarded sometimes rather than every time. Under certain conditions, this can produce persistent behavior. 

Suppose Avery is intensely warm one day and distant the next. Now attention has reason to remain vigilant. *Which Avery will appear today?* Warm Avery provides powerful relief because the cold period came first. The contrast itself can intensify the experience. That does not mean Avery is intentionally manipulating Riley. People can be inconsistent for many reasons.

The phone makes uncertainty portable. We have unprecedented access to signals without equivalent access to meaning. You can see that someone was online. You cannot see why they did not answer you. The result is a seemingly endless supply of data that does not settle the question you actually care about.

Fear and hope can run the same loop. Sometimes the loop is terrifying. *Avery is losing interest. Check.* One hour: *This is real. I know it.* The next: *I imagined everything.* This emotional whiplash can make limerence especially exhausting. It also explains why relief feels so powerful. After spending an hour imagining rejection, one warm message can feel almost euphoric.

Morgan shifts on the couch, changing the channel. Riley jumps, momentarily forgetting Morgan was there. The disparity between the internal storm and the quiet living room feels absurd. But the loop doesn't care about the room. It only cares about the next piece of data.

> 💡 **Micro-Observation Prompt:**
> *What is your checking buying you right now? Information, a sense of action, relief, or a reason to keep hoping?*

You do not need certainty to act. You can make decisions under uncertainty. Riley may believe: *I need to know whether Avery wants me before I can decide what to do.* Not necessarily. Riley can decide: *This pattern is hurting my relationship. I need distance.* None of those decisions requires reading Avery’s mind. The Hope Loop says: *One more piece of information.* Agency says: *I already know enough to decide what this pattern is costing me.*

### **Chapter 6: Attachment, Shame, and the Fear of Not Being Chosen**

At 3:16 on Thursday afternoon, Avery sends Riley a message.
*Going to be pretty offline this weekend. Family stuff. Hope you have a good one.*

There is nothing cruel in it. Nothing ambiguous, really. For someone else, the message might be thoughtful. For Riley, the first feeling is a drop. Then heat. Then a sudden need to understand. *Why tell me in advance? Family stuff? What does that mean?*

Within a minute, Riley’s body has responded to a weekend before the weekend has happened. This is where attachment language can help—if we use it carefully. Not as a personality quiz. As a way of understanding what happens when closeness starts to feel threatened.

Attachment theory began as an attempt to understand how children respond to caregivers, especially under conditions of threat, separation, and reunion. In adulthood, researchers often describe attachment using two broad dimensions: attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance. 

People higher in attachment anxiety are often more vigilant to signs of withdrawal and may use hyperactivating strategies under relational stress. *Hyperactivating* simply means turning the attachment system up. More attention. More rumination. More reassurance-seeking.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> The broad adult attachment literature finds that people higher in attachment anxiety may ruminate, catastrophize, or scrutinize cues of partner availability. *(PubMed Central)*

What direct limerence research actually says about attachment:

> 📊 **[Direct Limerence Evidence]**
> The 2025 Limerence Questionnaire study found that attachment anxiety was significantly associated with the questionnaire’s Intense Need for Attachment factor. But attachment anxiety was *not* significantly associated with its Neglect to Self and Others factor. Avoidant attachment was not significantly related to either dimension. *(Marshall et al., 2025)*

Attachment belongs in the conversation. It does not tell us limerence is one attachment style wearing romantic clothing. Attachment may explain why distance feels threatening. It does not necessarily explain why Avery became the person around whom the threat is organized.

If Riley has repeatedly experienced closeness as unreliable, Avery’s reduced availability may activate far more than disappointment. The body may respond to a much older prediction: *Connection is disappearing. Act now.* 

Seeking closeness is not inherently maladaptive. The problem arises when the system does not switch off after enough evidence of safety, or when the other person cannot or should not provide the level of reassurance being demanded. Then proximity-seeking can become self-defeating. The more frightened you become, the more contact you seek. The more pressure they feel, the more they pull back. Their withdrawal then seems to confirm the original fear. *See? People leave.*

**Hypervigilance does not mean accurate mind-reading**

Anxious attachment can create something that feels like extraordinary sensitivity. You notice tiny changes. Tone. Timing. Word choice. Whether someone reaches for you first. This vigilance can occasionally make people very perceptive. But vigilance is not the same as accuracy. 

Attention gives you data. Fear gives the data meaning. The goal of attachment work is not to convince yourself that all threatening cues are imaginary. It is to become better at tolerating the period between: *Something may have changed* and: *I know what it means.*

**Shame changes the stakes**

Attachment fear becomes especially painful when loss is interpreted as a verdict. There is an enormous difference between: *Avery does not want this relationship,* and: *Avery does not want this relationship because I am not worth wanting.* 

The first is painful information about a relationship. The second is a judgment about the self. That is shame. Shame collapses an event into identity. Rejected becomes *rejectable*. Mistake becomes *defective*. 

Once shame enters the loop, reassurance gains another psychological job. It does not merely prove that the relationship is intact. It proves that *you* are intact. No wonder silence hurts. The shame hears: *You are still worth keeping.*

We can describe one common pattern like this:
1. You carry a painful belief about yourself (*I am too much; I am forgettable*).
2. A person becomes unusually important. Their attention feels corrective.
3. Their availability changes. The old belief wakes up. Shame surges.
4. You seek more reassurance. You message. Check. Over-explain. Apologize repeatedly.
5. The other person becomes overwhelmed. They create distance.
6. Now the original belief has evidence. *I knew it.*

This is the cruel part of shame. It can help produce the very interpersonal conditions it fears. 

**Protest behavior: when fear comes out sideways**

Sometimes attachment fear looks like closeness-seeking. Sometimes it looks like anger. Silence. Jealousy. Punishment. A person feels: *Please prove I matter.* What comes out is: “Whatever. Do what you want.” They want reassurance. They create a test. *If they really care, they’ll notice I’m upset.*

These are sometimes called *protest behaviors*. The phrase can be useful, but it should not become an excuse. Your hidden need may be understandable. The behavior is still your responsibility. 

Morgan does not experience Riley’s coldness as: “A fascinating manifestation of attachment activation.” Morgan experiences Riley being cold. Inner mechanism and outer impact are both real. We will keep returning to that distinction.

**Self-compassion is not letting yourself off the hook**

People often resist self-compassion for exactly this reason. *If I stop attacking myself, won’t I excuse what happened?* Usually, self-compassion asks for something harder. It asks you to remain present. Shame offers escape through collapse. *I am horrible. End of analysis.* Self-compassion says: *I am a human being capable of causing harm, and I still have to look directly at what I did.* 

You need enough dignity to stay in the room with your own behavior. Otherwise, every mistake becomes intolerable. And what we cannot tolerate, we often avoid examining.

Another person’s autonomy is not abandonment. They can love you and need time alone. Care about you and make another friend. Be attracted to you and decide not to pursue it. Choose a different relationship. Set a boundary. Disappoint you. Security is not the absence of painful information. It is the growing ability to remain a whole person while another person remains separate.

Love does not erase the space between two people. It learns to live across it.

### **Chapter 7: The Person and the Projection**

Riley decides to do something simple. Describe Avery. No interpretations. No future. No statements about what Avery feels. Just Avery.

The first attempts do not go well.
“Avery is incredibly understanding.” *How does Riley know?* Because Avery listens closely and remembers things. Good. Observable.
“Avery really sees me.” *What does 'sees me' mean?* Avery asks questions about Riley’s writing and remembered the name of a story Riley mentioned months ago. Good.
“Avery and I have this connection that’s different.” *Different how?* Silence. “We just do.”

That sentence feels true. It may even be true. But it is not evidence. 

Projection does not mean “you made them up.” Avery exists. Avery really did ask about Riley’s writing. Projection happens when our own hopes, fears, needs, expectations, and assumptions help fill in what we cannot directly know. Every human relationship contains some of this. You never have direct access to another person’s mind. You infer. You interpret. You build a model of who they are. The question is how easily your model updates when reality disagrees.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Research on established romantic couples has repeatedly found that some degree of positive illusion—seeing a partner in an especially favorable light—can be associated with relationship satisfaction and stability. The problem with limerent idealization cannot simply be: *You see Avery positively.* The more useful questions are: How much of the picture has actually been tested? What happens when contrary evidence appears? *(PubMed Central)*

Fantasy has one enormous advantage over reality. It does not need complete data. Riley knows how Avery behaves during deadlines. Has heard stories about previous relationships. But Riley has never shared money with Avery; negotiated chores; handled long-term sexual mismatch; spent three years parenting with Avery; lived through a financial crisis together. That information does not exist. Yet the imagined relationship may already feel highly compatible. The mind has to simulate futures using incomplete information. The problem comes when simulation becomes evidence.

Two people can have breathtaking conversational chemistry and terrible conflict compatibility. Excellent sex and incompatible futures. A deep emotional bond and no workable romantic relationship. Limerence often struggles with that because mutual feeling seems as though it should logically require mutual action. Human relationships do not work that way. Attraction gives someone information about their feelings. It does not remove their right to choose what they do with those feelings.

**Reality–Story–Unknowns**

We now have enough groundwork for one of the central tools in this book. When you are activated, separate information into three categories.

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: REALITY–STORY–UNKNOWNS**
> 
> **Reality:** What can you observe? What was actually said? What behavior occurred?
> **Story:** What meaning have you added?
> **Unknown:** What information is genuinely unavailable?

For Riley:
*   **Reality:** Avery said, “I care about you, but I don’t want a romantic relationship.” Avery has continued sending friendly messages twice a week. Avery initiated lunch once.
*   **Story:** Avery is scared. Avery wants Riley but feels guilty. The lunch means Avery is reconsidering. The continued messages prove the bond is too strong to end.
*   **Unknown:** Whether Avery privately fantasizes about Riley. Whether Avery’s feelings might change. Why Avery initiated lunch. Whether Avery misses the previous level of closeness.

The goal is not: *Negative Story → Positive Story.* It is: *Story → recognize story.* Sometimes the responsible answer is: *I don’t know.* That can be deeply unsatisfying. It can also be the most truthful statement available.

**Example: The delayed reply**
*   **Reality:** Avery has not replied for six hours.
*   **Story:** Avery is distancing because Riley became too intense.
*   **Unknown:** Why Avery has not replied.
Notice what happens if Riley replaces the negative story with: “Avery is probably just busy.” That may calm Riley. It is still a story. A mature relationship with uncertainty does not require always selecting the most comforting explanation. It says: *Several explanations are possible. I do not currently know.*

**Example: The affectionate touch**
*   **Reality:** Avery put a hand on Riley’s back while walking through a crowded doorway.
*   **Story:** Avery wanted an excuse for physical contact.
*   **Unknown:** Avery’s intention.
Could Riley ask? Maybe. Would that be appropriate? Depends on context. But mentally replaying the touch sixty times does not turn the unknown into known.

You can let a meaningful moment be meaningful without turning it into a verdict on the future. 

**The pedestal is lonely for both people**

Imagine being Avery. At first, Riley’s attention may feel wonderful. Then Avery starts realizing that normal behavior produces large consequences. A delayed response hurts Riley. A new friendship creates jealousy. Being tired is interpreted as withdrawal. 

Now the flattering attention begins to feel like a job. Avery cannot simply be Avery. Avery has become the person who makes Riley feel alive. That is a tremendous role to carry. The more idealized someone becomes, the less room they may have to be ordinary. A pedestal is also a place where normal movement becomes dangerous. There is very little room to move.

**What if the fantasy really is better?**

There is an uncomfortable question worth taking seriously. What if Avery actually would be a better partner than Morgan? Possible. Limerence does not magically make every judgment wrong. 

Remove Avery from the evaluation as much as possible. Ask: *If Avery disappeared tomorrow, what would still be true about my relationship with Morgan? What problems would remain? What needs would still be unmet? Would I still believe this relationship needs significant change?* 

That question helps separate information revealed by the limerence from conclusions dependent upon the fantasy. Avery may have exposed a problem. Avery does not automatically determine the solution.

The fantasy may contain instructions. Instead of: *How do I make this scene happen with Avery?* ask: *What does this scene tell me I am hungry for?* That returns us to Chapter 4. Person. Symbol. Need. Now reality has a chance to enter. 

What I know. What I infer. What I hope. What I fear. What I need. What belongs to the other person. Those categories overlap. They should not collapse. Confusion becomes dangerous when one category starts making decisions for all the others.

### **Chapter 8: The Relationship That Quietly Gets Displaced**

Morgan does not know the word limerence. Morgan knows Riley has been somewhere else. 

Not literally. Riley still comes home. Still helps with dinner. Still asks whether the dog has been fed. Nothing obvious enough has happened for Morgan to point at it and say, *There. That’s the problem.* 

Instead, there are small absences. Riley smiles at the phone and then turns it facedown. Morgan tells a story and has to repeat the last sentence. A conversation that used to turn into twenty minutes now ends with “Yeah, totally.” Morgan has started asking: “Are we okay?” Riley keeps answering: “Of course.” This should be reassuring. It isn’t. 

When what you are told repeatedly conflicts with what you can feel happening beside you, you eventually start doubting one of two things: the relationship, or yourself.

This is one of the quieter ways limerence can affect an established partnership. No kiss is required. No sex. No dramatic declaration. A relationship can begin changing because finite parts of one person’s inner life have started moving somewhere else. 

That is what I mean by displacement. Something that once had one destination now has another. Time. Attention. Attunement. Truth. Future. 

**The Five Displacements**

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: THE FIVE DISPLACEMENTS**
> 
> 1. **Time:** The hours spent scrolling, rereading, fantasizing, and extending days to maintain proximity.
> 2. **Attention:** The body remains in the room, but the mind is monitoring the phone or replaying an interaction.
> 3. **Attunement:** The emotional contrast problem. The limerent object is charged; the partner is familiar. Curiosity and vulnerability go to the new person; the partner receives the leftovers.
> 4. **Truth:** Selective accuracy. Editing reality so the partner makes decisions using incomplete information.
> 5. **Future:** Privately comparing two futures while asking the current partner to make decisions as though nothing has changed.

**Displacement One: Time**
Time is the easiest resource to count and one of the easiest to minimize. Riley does not spend six continuous hours with Avery every night. Instead: twelve minutes scrolling; seven rereading messages; twenty-two lost in fantasy before sleep; ten checking whether Avery is online. Each piece looks small. Together, they become substantial. 

Attention has a recovery time. You can spend two minutes checking something and fifteen minutes emotionally responding to what you saw. Riley counts contact. Morgan lives with the aftermath.

**Displacement Two: Attention**
Attention is harder to measure because a body can remain in one place while the mind is elsewhere. Morgan receives enough attention for the conversation to technically continue. Not enough to feel fully met. 

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Relationship researchers use the term *perceived partner responsiveness* to describe the feeling that a close partner understands you, values what matters to you, and cares about your well-being. Morgan has begun reaching and finding a delay. *(ScienceDirect)*

**Displacement Three: Attunement**
Attention asks: *Are you here?* Attunement asks: *Are you with me?* Limerence can create an emotional contrast problem. Avery’s world is charged. Morgan’s world is familiar. Avery’s messages contain uncertainty and possibility. Morgan needs to discuss an insurance bill. If Riley gives curiosity, anticipation, imagination, vulnerability, and sexual attention primarily to Avery, then returns to Morgan with whatever is left, Morgan will eventually experience the relationship as depleted.

**Displacement Four: Truth**
Secrecy is complicated. Privacy is healthy. Secrecy is different when information is withheld because disclosure would change another person’s understanding of the relationship they are participating in. 

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> A 2023 longitudinal study found that people in lower-quality relationships tended to be more preoccupied with secrets they were keeping; it did not find clear evidence that secret preoccupation itself subsequently caused deterioration in trust. Sometimes secrecy damages relationships. Sometimes an already distant relationship makes secrecy more likely. *(Personal Relationships)*

Riley is editing reality. Morgan asks: “Did you talk to Avery today?” “Just at work.” True. They also messaged for two hours afterward. This form of dishonesty is not always built from outright lies. It can be built from selective accuracy. You answer the smallest version of the question.

**Displacement Five: Future**
The fifth displacement is the hardest to see because nothing concrete has to change. It happens in imagination. Morgan is still planning one future. Riley is privately comparing two. That asymmetry matters.

**The comparison no real partner can win**

Imagine two versions of Riley’s evening.
With Morgan: Morgan is tired. The kitchen needs cleaning. They disagree about whether to spend money on something. There is affection. There is also history.
With Avery: Riley sees Avery for forty minutes. They laugh. There are no bills. Nobody is asking Riley to unload the dishwasher. Then they separate before either has enough time to become irritating. 

Riley goes home thinking: *Why can’t it feel this easy with Morgan?* Because Riley is comparing two different conditions. One relationship carries ordinary life. The other is still being protected from it. Before Riley makes major decisions based on that contrast, Riley needs to know what is actually being compared.

**When reassurance stops landing**

Riley says: “I love you.” Morgan believes it. And somehow it no longer solves the problem. The problem is not a shortage of sentences. It is a mismatch between sentences and pattern. 

You say: “You’re my priority.” But cancel dinner because Avery might be available. Words matter. Behavior teaches. When they conflict, people eventually learn from behavior. 

Trust is built in the boring middle. You said you would be home at six. You are. You said you would stop hiding contact. You stop. Trust grows when another person’s nervous system receives enough repeated evidence that the old prediction no longer fits. Riley may understand the limerence in three weeks. Morgan may still be hurt six months later. Insight does not obligate another person to recover at the speed of the person who gained it.

### **Chapter 9: What It Feels Like to Become Someone’s Fantasy**

At first, Avery likes the attention. Why wouldn’t they? Riley listens in a way that makes people feel interesting. Remembers tiny details. Seemed genuinely happy when Avery walks into the room. 

There is warmth between them. Maybe attraction too. Avery is not pretending otherwise. That matters. This is not a story in which one person imagines an entire relationship with a stranger who has shown no interest. There is something real here. That is what makes the next part harder.

Avery gradually begins noticing that ordinary behavior no longer feels ordinary around Riley. If Avery replies quickly, Riley brightens. If Avery takes longer, Riley asks whether everything is okay. If Avery mentions spending time with somebody else, something changes in Riley’s face. 

Avery starts reassuring before reassurance has been requested. “Just tired. Nothing you did.” The first few times, it feels thoughtful. Then Avery realizes something: *I am managing the meaning of everything I do.*

**Being intensely seen is not always the same as being known**

The problem is not being noticed. The problem is what happens when attention stops serving curiosity and starts serving prediction. Riley is no longer merely interested in Avery. Riley studies Avery. From Riley’s side, this may feel like extraordinary sensitivity. From Avery’s side, it can begin to feel like being under observation. You are present as a person. You are also being used as information.

The pedestal changes shape. A pedestal sounds positive. But a pedestal is also a place where normal movement becomes dangerous. The version of you that lives in the other person’s mind can become difficult to inhabit. You are expected to remain warm enough to preserve their belief. Available enough to protect them from uncertainty. No person can do this indefinitely.

**The pressure to perform**

Avery starts thinking before replying. Not: *What do I want to say?* But: *How will Riley take this?* If Avery is too warm, will Riley read it romantically? If too brief, will Riley spiral? The relationship gradually acquires a performance requirement. Avery has to be responsible not only for what Avery means, but for how Riley might interpret it. This is too much responsibility for one person.

When kindness becomes a contract you never signed. Avery knows Riley is hurting. The natural impulse is compassion. Maybe Avery sends a reassuring message. Riley feels better. Avery feels relieved. Then the same need returns. This is where compassion can quietly turn into a job. 

The guilt trap. Guilt can be healthy. If you harm someone, guilt may tell you that your behavior violated your values. But people can also feel guilty for causing pain when they have done nothing wrong. Avery says: “I don’t want to text every night anymore.” Riley cries. Avery feels guilty. But what is the guilt claiming? That Riley’s pain proves the boundary is wrong? It does not. 

Avery can care about Riley’s pain without becoming responsible for eliminating it. That is the difference between compassion and obligation. Compassion says: *I care that this hurts you.* Obligation says: *Because this hurts you, I must give you what you need from me.*

**Boundary fatigue**

Avery says: “I can’t talk tonight.” Riley asks why. Avery explains. Riley asks whether something is wrong. Avery reassures. Riley asks whether Avery is upset. Twenty minutes later, the original boundary—I can’t talk tonight—has required a full emotional processing session. The next time Avery is tired, it may feel easier just to answer. This is how boundaries can erode without anyone explicitly saying: “You are not allowed to have limits.”

When concern becomes fear. Most people experiencing limerence do not become threatening. Intrusive thoughts are not the same as intrusive behavior. But some patterns can escalate.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Researchers use the term *obsessive relational intrusion* (ORI) to describe repeated pursuit of intimacy with someone who does not want that level or type of intimacy. Stalking is related but generally involves a repeated course of unwanted behavior associated with fear, threat, or substantial distress. *(Wiley / ScienceDirect)*

Digital life makes this line easy to blur. There is a difference between seeing a public post that happens to appear in your feed and organizing your day around monitoring someone’s online activity. Technology can turn emotional vigilance into actual surveillance. 

> ⚠️ **[Safety Warning]**
> The U.S. Department of Justice explicitly recognizes technological monitoring, tracking, repeated unwanted messaging, and social-media monitoring as behaviors that can occur in stalking and abuse contexts. Looking at an ex’s Instagram after a breakup does not automatically make someone a stalker. But if the behavior becomes repeated, unwanted, invasive, frightening, or designed to bypass another person’s attempt to create distance, it has crossed into a different ethical territory. Your pain does not authorize access.

Avery gets to exist outside the story. The person you are fixated on has an interior life that does not belong to your narrative. Avery’s silence may have nothing to do with Riley. Avery’s boundary is not a puzzle designed to reveal hidden desire. Recovery gradually restores something simpler: *They are a person having a life.*

### **Chapter 10: Boundaries, Responsibility, and When Intensity Stops Being Romantic**

Avery waits until Friday afternoon. The message takes nearly an hour to write. 

*I care about you, and I need our contact to change. I can’t keep texting throughout the day or talking about your feelings for me. I want to be respectful, but this has become too intense for me. Please don’t contact me outside work for now.*

Riley reads it once. Then again. Then a third time. The body reacts before thought catches up. Chest tight. Face hot. Hands cold. 

The first thought is not generous. *After everything?* Then: *Avery is abandoning me.* Then: *There has to be another explanation.* Riley starts typing. Stops. Starts again. 

What happens next matters. Not because Riley’s pain is fake. Because Avery has said something clear. This is the point where the central question changes from: *How do I feel?* to: *What will I do with how I feel?*

**A boundary is not an argument you have to win**

A useful boundary identifies a limit around what a person can participate in, tolerate, provide, or allow access to. Avery says: “I can’t keep texting throughout the day.” That is a boundary. Riley says: “You aren’t allowed to date anyone else because it triggers me.” That is an attempt to control Avery’s behavior.

> 📐 **THE BIMERENT BEHAVIOR VS. ABUSE BOUNDARY TEST**
> 
> **Pain is limerence.** Feeling devastated, abandoned, panicked, or unable to sleep because someone set a boundary is a feature of the psychological state. It deserves compassion.
> 
> **Coercion is abuse.** Using your pain to override another person's 'no'—whether through repeated messaging, guilt-tripping, threats of self-harm, showing up uninvited, or digital surveillance—is crossing the line into abusive behavior. It requires immediate cessation and accountability.

Your emotional activation does not automatically give you authority over somebody else’s choices. You can hurt more than the other person understands. You can feel abandoned. Panicked. Humiliated. You can know that distance will devastate you. And the other person may still say no. 

Your pain matters. It does not create entitlement. Not to a reply. Not to access. Not to another conversation. Not to reassurance. Not to friendship. Not even to the closure you believe would make all of this easier. If pain created entitlement, the person in the most distress would always have the most power. That is not compassion. It is captivity.

**A boundary can be loving and still feel terrible**

If Avery were cruel, leaving might be easier. Instead Avery says: “I care about you,” and: “I can’t keep doing this.” Riley’s mind wants those statements to cancel each other out. *If you care, stay. If you leave, you never cared.* 

That is black-and-white logic created by pain. Real relationships are full of statements that coexist uncomfortably. *I love you, and I cannot be your partner. I care about you, and I need distance.* Maturity is not making one side disappear. Respect does not require certainty about the future. It requires responding to what the person has actually said in the present.

**When self-harm enters the boundary**

This is one of the most serious situations in intense attachment dynamics. Suppose Avery says the relationship has to end. Riley says: “I can’t survive this.” Then: “If you leave, I don’t know what I’ll do to myself.” Now Avery is holding something terrifying. 

> ⚠️ **[Safety Warning]**
> The National Domestic Violence Hotline specifically identifies threats of suicide during arguments among behaviors that can function as emotional abuse in coercive relationships. U.S. stalking guidance also recognizes suicide threats as one form of intimidation. A person can be genuinely suicidal. A person can also communicate genuine suicidality in a way that places coercive pressure on someone else. Both can be true. If someone says they may harm themselves, treat the safety concern as real. Involve appropriate human support. What you should not do is establish the rule: *I must remain in this relationship or this person might die.* 

If you are the person having the self-harm urge, your pain deserves immediate support. But the person whose boundary triggered the pain cannot safely become your only crisis plan. Reach outward. Not only toward them.

**When self-attack becomes avoidance**

Shame says: *I am a terrible person.* Guilt can motivate repair. Shame often collapses attention inward. Now Riley needs Morgan to provide reassurance: “No, you’re not awful.” The injured person suddenly becomes responsible for comforting the person whose behavior hurt them. This is one reason self-attack can look like accountability while functioning as avoidance. Specific behavior creates specific responsibility.

**Responsibility is behavioral**

People often become trapped in discussions of intention. Intention provides context. Responsibility asks what happens next. 

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: IMPACT BEFORE STORY**
> 
> **What happened?** (The behavior, stripped of adjectives).
> **What did it cost?** (The impact on the other person).
> **What will change?** (The specific behavioral correction).
> **Then, what explains the pattern?** (The psychological context, if useful).
> 
> *Example: "I kept asking you to explain after you had already said you needed space. I can see that this made your boundary feel negotiable and forced you to keep defending it. You do not owe me another explanation. I will stop contacting you outside the circumstances you agreed to."*

Notice what is absent. A fifteen-minute explanation of attachment wounds. A plea for Avery to confirm Riley is still a good person. A demand for forgiveness. Those things may matter elsewhere. They do not belong before impact.

If you have clearly said “No,” and the person repeatedly argues, bargains, finds new routes of contact, monitors you, threatens you, threatens themselves in order to prevent separation, appears unexpectedly, or makes you afraid, the problem is no longer that you have not communicated clearly enough. Your next task is not to become more persuasive. It is to protect your safety.

> ⚠️ **[Safety Warning]**
> In the United States, the Department of Justice defines stalking as a course of conduct directed at a specific person that would cause a reasonable person to fear for safety or experience substantial emotional distress. Legal definitions differ by jurisdiction. You do not need to settle the legal label before seeking help. If you are afraid, take the fear seriously. Tell someone. Document concerning behavior where safe. Seek local professional or law-enforcement support.

What love looks like when you cannot have access. Can you care about someone without requiring access to them? Can you miss them without monitoring them? Can you be devastated by their boundary and still protect it? Sometimes care looks like leaving another person alone. Sometimes love becomes ethical precisely when it stops demanding to be rewarded.

### **Chapter 11: Mapping Your Own Loop**

On Monday morning, Riley gets to work before Avery. This should make the day easier. Instead, Riley notices Avery’s empty chair. There is the first shift. Not panic. Just awareness. 

* Avery is usually here by now.
* Riley opens email. Reads three sentences. Looks toward the empty chair again. 
* Maybe Avery is working from home. Did Avery tell me that? Riley searches memory. No. The body becomes slightly more alert.

Nothing dramatic has happened. No rejection. No argument. No message. A chair is empty. Still, an entire sequence has begun.

By the time you notice yourself doing the behavior you want to stop, several important things may already have happened. There was a trigger. Then a meaning. Then a change in your body. Then an urge. Only after that came the action. If all of those events collapse into one sentence—*I saw Avery wasn’t there and checked the calendar*—you miss most of the places where the pattern can change.

A trigger is an event that activates a response. It is not necessarily the deeper cause of the response. The chair did not create Riley’s fear of losing the relationship. A recovery plan that works only when nothing activates you is not much of a recovery plan.

**The Trigger-to-Need Map**

We are going to use one framework repeatedly from here forward.

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: TRIGGER-TO-NEED MAP**
> 
> `Trigger → Interpretation → Body State → Urge → Behavior → Immediate Payoff → Longer-Term Cost → Need`
> 
> *This is an adaptation of principles used in functional behavioral analysis and behavior-chain work. Dialectical behavior therapy, for example, uses chain analysis to examine the sequence of events, vulnerabilities, thoughts, feelings, and actions surrounding problematic behavior rather than treating the behavior as an isolated failure.* *(PubMed)*

Let’s walk through Riley’s Monday.
* **Trigger:** Avery’s chair is empty. Just the event.
* **Interpretation:** Avery may be avoiding me. / I may be losing access.
* **Body state:** Tension. Restlessness. Alertness.
* **Urge:** Get information.
* **Behavior:** Check Avery’s calendar. Then messages. Then online status.
* **Immediate payoff:** Riley discovers Avery has a dentist appointment. Relief.
* **Longer-term cost:** The mind learns: When Avery’s absence creates uncertainty, checking is how we become okay again.
* **Need:** Security. Reassurance. Predictability.

Your interpretation is often where the emotional volume changes. Suppose the trigger is: *They do not reply for six hours.*
Interpretation A: *Busy day.* The body may remain fairly calm.
Interpretation B: *They’re pulling away.* More activation.
Interpretation C: *I knew this would happen. I got attached and now I’m being abandoned again.* The present event has now become connected to an entire history.

**The body deserves its own line**

People often skip the body when analyzing limerence. That is a mistake. An urge can feel mentally convincing partly because it is physically uncomfortable. The chest tightens. The stomach drops. You cannot settle. If you want to change the action, you need another way to live through the body state. Regulation before action. 

Trying to debate romantic compatibility while highly activated is like trying to reorganize a closet during a fire alarm. The first intervention may be surprisingly physical. Feet on the floor. Walking. Slower breathing. Cold water on the face. Not because these exercises solve limerence. They make it easier not to let one activated minute decide what happens next.

**The urge is not the need**

Riley feels: *I need to text Avery.* Maybe not. Riley has an urge to text Avery. Underneath the urge may be a need for security; connection; clarity; comfort; recognition; relief. An urge proposes one method for meeting a need. It is not the need itself. 

If you are lonely, the urge says: Check their Instagram. Instagram does not meet the need for belonging. If you are ashamed, the urge says: Get them to reassure you. Reassurance may temporarily soften shame. It does not build stable self-worth. 

What problem is this urge promising to solve? Sometimes the answer is practical. Send the message. Sometimes the answer is emotional. That cannot usually be settled by one message.

**Trigger families**

It is more useful to group triggers into broader families.
*   **Distance:** Less contact. A delayed response.
*   **Ambiguity:** Mixed messages. Warmth followed by silence.
*   **Rejection:** A no. Criticism. A boundary.
*   **Competition:** A new partner. Another friend.
*   **Autonomy:** The other person making decisions you do not control.
*   **Shame:** Feeling foolish. Too intense. Ordinary.
*   **Novelty:** A fresh spark. A sudden intimate moment.
*   **Inconsistency:** Warm then distant.

The goal is not to memorize a taxonomy. It is to discover which situations reliably begin your chain. One full map is worth more than fifty vague insights. 

Some needs require people. When you reach the “need” part of the map, do not automatically decide the answer should come entirely from yourself. If the need is connection, call someone. If you need comfort, seek healthy comfort. Recovery from limerence should not become an ideology of total self-sufficiency. People heal in relationship too. The goal is: *I need more than one route to stability, and I need routes that do not violate anyone’s autonomy or keep strengthening the fixation.*

### **Chapter 12: Learning Not to Feed the Loop**

At 7:28 that evening, Riley reaches for the phone. The movement is almost automatic. Avery’s profile is one tap away. 

Riley stops. Nothing dramatic happens. There is no sudden insight. No surge of empowerment. The urge remains. That matters. People often imagine that a good coping skill makes the urge disappear. More often, a skill does something less impressive and more useful: It helps you avoid turning the urge into behavior. 

Riley puts the phone down. The mind immediately argues. *Just once. You already made it this far. What if something actually happened?* Riley notices how quickly the mind can generate a reason. 

You already know Avery is partly real and partly projected. Insight matters. Insight does not automatically stop your hand. Behavior change needs its own practice.

**The goal is not thought control**

You do not need to reach a state in which the person never enters your mind. Thoughts happen. The useful measure is what happens next. Do you spend an hour elaborating the thought? Check? Message? Or can the thought exist without acquiring the rest of your evening?

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Psychological flexibility is the ability to remain in contact with difficult thoughts and feelings while still behaving in ways that serve your values. Research on Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) supports psychological flexibility and reductions in processes such as cognitive fusion and experiential avoidance as meaningful mechanisms of change. *(PubMed)*

**The Urge Window**

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: THE URGE WINDOW**
> 
> `Notice → Name → Regulate → Delay → Choose`
> 
> 1. **Notice:** There is the urge.
> 2. **Name:** Checking urge. Contact urge. Fantasy urge. Naming changes the grammar. "I need to check" becomes "I am having an urge to check."
> 3. **Regulate:** Do something that helps reduce the sense of emergency.
> 4. **Delay:** Set a period during which you will not act. Five minutes at first. Then ten.
> 5. **Choose:** After the delay, ask, *What action serves my values rather than just reducing this feeling?*

Urges are waves, not commands. An urge can spike. Argue. Return. You may feel worse before you feel better. The useful discovery is not that every urge disappears quickly. It is that you can experience an urge without completing the usual response.

**Response prevention: useful principle, limited direct limerence evidence**

Exposure and response prevention (ERP) is a well-established first-line psychological treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder. The urge appears. You do not perform the ritual. You learn that distress can change without checking. However, ERP has not been established through clinical trials as a standard treatment for limerence. 

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> A 2021 limerence case study used cognitive-behavioral techniques including response prevention and reported reduced compulsive rituals and dysfunctional thinking at nine-month follow-up. That is encouraging; it is still a single case study, not proof of efficacy across a population. *(PubMed)*

If you have OCD or suspect that you do, formal ERP should be guided by an OCD-informed clinician rather than self-prescribed from a limerence book.

Stop solving uncertainty with rituals. Consider how many behaviors may function as rituals even when they do not look clinical. Checking whether they viewed your story. Reviewing the last conversation. Searching their new partner. Asking a friend: “Do you think they like me?” Drafting a message repeatedly. 

Make the behavior harder. We often overestimate willpower and underestimate friction. If Avery’s profile is available in one tap, the urge only needs to win for half a second. Create friction. Mute. Unfollow. Archive the message thread. Disable active-status visibility. Environmental design can feel almost embarrassingly basic compared with the emotional complexity of limerence. Use it anyway. Changing the environment is not cheating. It is behavior design.

**No contact is not a religion**

Contact exists in different contexts. An ex-partner. A coworker. A coparent. A person who has explicitly asked you not to contact them. Those situations are not interchangeable. The useful question is: *What does contact do to the loop?* If every interaction produces three days of rumination, contact may be too expensive right now. 

Fantasy needs response prevention too. You can block Avery everywhere and still maintain an entire relationship internally. The target is not imagination. It is compulsive or prolonged elaboration that keeps the attachment emotionally active against your own goals. 

*Avery thought.* Then return to the current task. Not angrily. More like noticing a familiar road and deciding not to turn down it.

**Values are not goals**

A goal is: *Do not contact Avery for thirty days.* A value is: *Respect. Integrity. Presence.* A goal can be completed. A value is a direction. Suppose day twenty-two goes badly and Riley sends a message. The goal is broken. The value is still available five minutes later. Riley can stop. Own it. Re-establish the boundary. Values prevent one mistake from becoming: *I failed, so nothing matters.*

What do you want your behavior to stand for? Imagine Avery never knows about your recovery. No applause. No final conversation. What do you want your behavior to stand for anyway? That question moves recovery away from performance.

A lapse is not a collapse. You do well for two weeks. Then check. The shame voice says: *Nothing changed.* A lapse is an event. Collapse is what happens when the event becomes a story: *I am incapable of change.*

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Research on self-compassion-focused interventions finds meaningful reductions in self-criticism across randomized studies. "I checked. That strengthened the loop. I understand why I wanted relief. I am going to stop now and look at what made tonight harder." Compassion without accountability becomes avoidance. Accountability without compassion becomes shame. *(PubMed)*

The empty space is the next problem. Eventually, there is less checking. And then a surprisingly difficult question appears: *What do I do with all this space?* You cannot build a life out of: *don’t check; don’t fantasize; don’t message.* Something has to return.

### **Chapter 13: Building a Life That Does Not Depend on Being Chosen**

The first Saturday after Riley stops checking Avery’s social media is terrible. Not dangerous. Just empty. Riley wakes up and reaches for the phone. Remembers. Makes coffee. Morgan is still asleep. The house is quiet. For months, quiet mornings contained Avery. Now there is coffee. A kitchen. A dog staring hopefully at the back door. Riley feels almost cheated. This was supposed to feel like freedom. Instead, ordinary life seems painfully ordinary.

When limerence has been carrying novelty, erotic charge, hope, fantasy, companionship, validation, and escape, reducing the fixation creates a real psychological vacancy. You have not only stopped a habit. You may have removed one of your strongest sources of reward. If recovery ends there, the old loop remains extremely attractive.

Recovery by subtraction is fragile. Durable recovery needs another center. Values. Relationships. Work worth doing. Play. Pleasure. Community. Identity.

**Distributed Need Fulfillment**

Earlier, we used the phrase Person / Symbol / Need. Now we add a fourth category: Alternative Sources.

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: DISTRIBUTED NEED FULFILLMENT**
> 
> A healthy life spreads important needs across multiple relationships, activities, capacities, and sources of meaning rather than asking one person to carry the entire psychological load.
> 
> *   **Relatedness:** Partner, friends, family, community, support groups, chosen family.
> *   **Novelty:** Travel, learning, new environments, creative work, shared adventures.
> *   **Recognition:** Friendship, work, art, mentorship, your own ability to recognize yourself.
> *   **Erotic vitality:** Partner, dating, sexual communication, your own sexuality, embodiment.
> *   **Competence:** Work, craft, fitness, parenting, learning, building something difficult.
> *   **Autonomy:** Time alone, boundaries, independent friendships, money, creative ownership.

One person can participate in many of these. No one person should be forced to carry all of them.

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Self-determination theory has decades of research around three basic psychological needs: autonomy (meaningful choice), competence (feeling effective), and relatedness (feeling connected). Large meta-analytic work supports strong links between environments that support these needs and well-being. *(PubMed)*

> 💡 **Micro-Observation Prompt: The Life Ecosystem Canvas**
> *Draw a circle divided into wedges: Partner, Friends, Work, Hobbies, Solitude, Community. Shade in how much of your emotional energy is currently going into each wedge. If one person's name answers most of your needs, your life is carrying concentration risk. Diversification is not emotional distance. It is resilience.*

**Aliveness is a need worth taking seriously**

Riley kept saying: “Avery makes me feel alive.” What makes Riley feel alive besides Avery? The answer is uncomfortable. Riley does not know anymore. 

Sometimes long-term adults become efficient at maintaining life and terrible at inhabiting it. Routine keeps households functioning. But if every source of novelty and challenge disappears, a new person can arrive carrying an enormous amount of contrast. 

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> The self-expansion model proposes that people are motivated to grow through new experiences. Shared novel or challenging activities can support relationship quality and well-being, especially in long-term couples. *(PubMed Central)*

Riley can ask: *Where has expansion disappeared from my life?* Perhaps Riley starts writing again. Perhaps Riley and Morgan try something new together. But do not turn your partner into the anti-limerence treatment. You realize Avery represents excitement. Then you go to Morgan and demand: “We need to be more spontaneous.” Morgan may reasonably think: *I have been carrying the household while you spent six months emotionally elsewhere, and now I am responsible for becoming more exciting?* Some needs belong to the couple. Some belong to you. Some belong in friendship. 

**Rebuilding ordinary reward**

> 📝 **[Adjacent Field Evidence]**
> Behavioral activation is a well-established treatment approach for depression that works partly by increasing engagement with meaningful or rewarding activities rather than waiting for motivation to appear first. Action can precede desire. *(PubMed)*

Riley does not feel like going to a friend’s cookout. Goes anyway. Does not feel like writing. Opens the document. The first few attempts may not compare favorably with Avery. That is fine. The goal is to restore access to ordinary reinforcement: pleasure, mastery, connection, meaning. Over time, a life made of many moderate sources of reward is more stable than a life waiting for one enormous one.

Competence: becoming someone you can rely on. Do something. Complete something. Keep a promise to yourself. Competence creates a different kind of self-esteem. Not: *Avery thinks I am interesting.* But: *I know how to do this.* 

Relatedness: do not confuse independence with healing. There is a version of recovery culture that praises complete detachment. *Need nobody. Validate yourself.* Taken too far, it becomes another defense. Humans need relatedness. The goal is to stop making one person’s uncertain availability the narrow gate through which nearly all belonging must pass.

**If you are in a partnership, rebuild reality there**

Morgan and Riley have decisions to make. Does the relationship continue? This work cannot be: “How do we make Morgan as exciting as Avery?” It should be: “What kind of relationship do Morgan and Riley actually want now?” Maybe they need more conversation. More separate lives. More sex. Less sex and more affection. Couples therapy. Or perhaps they discover the relationship should end. The ethical requirement is to stop using the third person as the only measuring tool. Morgan deserves to be evaluated as Morgan.

Erotic life needs somewhere to go. Some limerence is deeply sexual. Ignoring this can make recovery feel like self-amputation. If Riley remains with Morgan, perhaps this becomes a conversation about their erotic relationship. If Riley is single, erotic energy can still belong to Riley without requiring Avery. What qualities are erotic in the fantasy? Being wanted? Novelty? Power? Secrecy? Those themes contain information. Information can be integrated without making the original fantasy literal.

Creativity can metabolize what contact cannot. Write. Make music. Draw. Build. Do not create as an indirect message to Avery. Create because emotional intensity can become form. A poem is not the same as sending the message. A story can hold something the relationship cannot.

Grief needs room inside the rebuilt life. You can go to dinner with friends and cry afterward. You can have a meaningful week and still miss someone. Healing does not require every new good thing to cancel an old loss. Life gets larger enough to hold both.

### **Chapter 14: Repair, Release, and the Long Middle**

Riley apologizes to Morgan on a Thursday. The conversation lasts nearly two hours. There are tears. Specific admissions. A few questions Riley wishes Morgan had not asked. At the end, Riley feels lighter. Morgan does not. 

This is the long middle. The part after insight and before outcome. Real repair is repetitive. Grief is repetitive. Trust is repetitive. You may know exactly what happened and still wake up the next morning with consequences. That is not failure. That is reality catching up more slowly than insight.

**Impact Before Story**

> 📐 **FRAMEWORK: IMPACT BEFORE STORY**
> 
> When you have harmed someone, begin with what happened in their reality before explaining the mechanism in yours.
> 
> **What happened?** "I kept telling you nothing was happening while I was spending hours thinking about Avery and hiding how much contact we had."
> **What did it cost?** "You were trying to understand why I felt absent and I made you question your own perception."
> **What will change?** "I will stop hiding my phone and be honest about my interactions."
> **Then, what explains the pattern?** "I have been trying to understand how I got there so I don't repeat it."

A good apology cannot force trust. Morgan can appreciate Riley’s apology. And still check whether Riley’s behavior changes. This is rational. Trust is partly a prediction. When that prediction has been violated, the other person needs new data. Not only emotion. Data.

Repair is behavioral evidence over time. Riley does not demand praise each time. If every act of trustworthy behavior comes with: “Did you notice?” the behavior becomes another request for reassurance. The injured person is allowed to remain uncertain. Being injured does not justify becoming abusive, but early in repair, expecting perfect emotional composure from the injured person can become another way of making Riley’s comfort the priority.

**Wanting repair and being ready for repair are different**

You can desperately want to save a relationship and still not currently have the capacity to do the work required. Perhaps you are sleeping three hours a night. Still checking constantly. Still lying. A responsible pause is better than promising work you cannot yet sustain. 

Morgan may say no. You can do everything right after doing something harmful and still lose the relationship. Repair requires two participants. You control your side. Growth cannot become another bargaining chip. *I did all this work. You have to stay.* No. Do the work because you want to become the person the work produces. Not because it guarantees an outcome.

Avery may not come back either. Respect was not a transaction. You did not purchase future access by behaving appropriately. True release gradually includes the possibility that there will be no reward. No reunion. The life still has to be worth living.

**Grieving a person who was never fully yours**

Limerence can create unusual grief because the loss may be difficult to explain socially. *But you weren’t even together.* Do not add shame to loss. You may grieve Avery. The friendship. The possibility. The fantasy. The version of yourself around Avery. The future you imagined. 

Grief is not evidence you made the wrong decision. You create distance. Then ache. The mind says: *This proves Avery mattered too much to let go.* No. Grief proves loss matters. People grieve necessary endings. Pain does not tell you whether a boundary is wise. It tells you that the boundary has a cost. Sometimes the right decision is expensive.

Do not turn grief into contact. You miss Avery. That becomes: *I should tell Avery I miss them.* If Avery asked for distance, the grief belongs somewhere else. Journal. Friend. Therapist. Write the message and do not send it. Not every true feeling needs to be delivered to the person it concerns. Containment is not dishonesty. It is recognizing that truth and access are separate things.

**What recurrence actually looks like**

Months later, Riley meets someone new. Jordan. There is a spark. Conversation runs long. Riley drives home energized. Then, while brushing teeth, realizes: *I’ve already imagined seeing Jordan again four times.* Panic. *Oh no.* 

This is the moment many people misinterpret. They think recovery meant becoming immune. That is not the goal. Riley is allowed to like Jordan. The question is whether old processes take over. Recurrence gives you an opportunity to use the map earlier. That is progress. 

Recovery is not: *Never activated. Never attracted. Never fantasy-prone.* A more realistic test is: *How quickly do I recognize the pattern? Can I tolerate the uncertainty? Can I let another person remain separate? Can I tell the truth?* These are functional markers. They are much more useful than emotional purity.

Repair with yourself. Limerence can damage your relationship with your own trust. *I can’t trust my feelings. My judgment is terrible.* Be careful. The lesson is not that your emotions are unreliable garbage. Your feelings contained real information. The problem was asking feelings to answer questions they could not answer. Intensity could tell you: *This matters to me.* It could not necessarily tell you: *This relationship is right for me.* Repairing self-trust means giving each form of information the job it can actually perform. That is wisdom, not distrust.

Release is not erasure. Avery may remain important to Riley for years. Occasionally a dream opens the old world for a few minutes. That does not mean release failed. Release means Avery is no longer required to become something else. Riley can carry memory without carrying pursuit. 

The long middle eventually becomes ordinary life. The strangest thing about recovery is how unremarkable it eventually becomes. There is no final ceremony. One day you realize you went three days without checking. Then you hear their name and notice your body react less. You stop measuring. You become busy with things that are actually yours.

### **Conclusion: When They Stop Being the Center of the Story**

It is another Tuesday night. Months have passed. The room looks almost exactly the same as it did at the beginning. Laundry near the stairs. Dog asleep badly across two cushions. A television show Riley is only half interested in. A phone on the coffee table. 

There is a message on it. Not from Avery. Riley noticed Avery’s name earlier today. Someone mentioned a project. The body reacted. A small movement. Not nothing. A memory came with it. Avery laughing. A particular afternoon. For a few seconds, another life was nearby again. Riley let it be there. Then the conversation continued.

That may not sound like a dramatic ending. It is the ending I trust.

**The goal was never indifference**

If you began this book wanting to become completely indifferent to the person, perhaps that happened. For other people, that is not what happens. You may still care. Still find them beautiful. Still occasionally wonder what another life might have looked like. 

The goal was never to force one particular emotional ending. The goal was choice. Choice when the thought arrives. Choice when uncertainty appears. Choice when a boundary hurts. Choice when fantasy offers escape. Choice when shame says you need someone else to prove you are worth keeping. That is what has changed.

**The person was never the whole story**

At the beginning, the problem looked like Avery. *If only Avery replied. If only Avery chose Riley.* Over time, the frame became larger. Avery mattered. But Avery had also become connected to recognition, freedom, aliveness, sexuality, hope, escape, worth, belonging. This did not make the attachment fake. It made the attachment understandable. 

The task was never to expose Avery as an illusion. It was to stop asking Avery to carry needs that belonged to an entire life. The Hope Loop was powerful because it worked in the short term. You were not irrational for repeating something that reduced distress. But short-term relief and long-term freedom are not always produced by the same behavior. Recovery asked you to tolerate the difference. To feel a question without answering it immediately. To feel an urge without obeying it. That is difficult work. It is also learnable.

**Reality did not require cruelty**

Reality–Story–Unknowns gave us another option. *What happened? What did you interpret? What can you genuinely not know?* Sometimes reality was beautiful. Avery cared. Sometimes reality was painful. Avery said no. Sometimes both were true. You did not have to make Avery cruel in order to respect the boundary. You did not have to decide the connection was fake in order to stop feeding it.

Accountability did not require self-hatred. Partners can be displaced. People can become pressured by idealization. Boundaries can be worn down. Secrecy can distort another person’s reality. Those truths mattered. So did another truth: Shame is a poor repair strategy. Specific behavior creates specific responsibility. Explanation came after reality. Not instead of it.

**You were never supposed to meet every need alone**

We also corrected another cultural fantasy. The fantasy of perfect self-sufficiency. You do not recover by becoming someone who needs nobody. You recover by creating enough internal stability, relational diversity, meaningful activity, and respect for autonomy that no one person’s attention has to hold the entire structure together. This was Distributed Need Fulfillment. Some needs belong inside you. Some are met with friends. Some with partners. Some in community. Some remain unmet for a while and you learn that unmet does not mean unbearable.

**Your feelings are information, not instructions**

Perhaps this is the sentence to keep. Your feelings are information. They tell you: *This person matters. I am afraid. I am lonely. I want. I am jealous. I feel alive. I am grieving.* They do not automatically tell you: *Contact them. Stay. Leave. Check. They are your soulmate. Your marriage is wrong. You are worthless. Their boundary is unfair. The future will happen.* 

Feelings are part of the evidence. They are not the entire verdict. Learning that distinction does not make you less romantic. It makes you more capable of loving another person as a person rather than as an answer.

**When they become a person again**

Perhaps the deepest change is this. At the height of limerence, the LO can become enormous. Every action means something. They become symbol, regulator, future, judge, rescuer, wound. Then gradually the scale returns. Avery becomes Avery. One human being. Capable of beauty. Contradiction. Kindness. Selfishness. Warmth. Distance. Choice. A private interior Riley cannot own. 

There is something sad about that shrinking. There is also relief. No person should have to be that large. Not for you. Not for themselves. And perhaps you should not have to live in a world where one person’s attention is large enough to determine whether you are okay.

The question has changed. At the beginning of this book, Riley’s mind kept asking: *What does this mean about whether Avery wants me?* That question organized everything. By the end, a different question is available: *What do I want my life to stand for whether Avery wants me or not?* 

That question is harder in one way. There is no single person who can answer it for you. It is also freer. Because the answer can be distributed across hundreds of ordinary choices. The message you do not send. The boundary you respect. The friend you call. The truth you tell. The relationship you repair. The relationship you release. The work you return to. The pleasure you allow yourself. The unknown you leave unknown. The life you stop postponing. 

Longing may still visit. It no longer has to run the house. And the person you could not stop thinking about can finally take their place in a story that is larger than them. 

Your own.

### **Frameworks at a Glance**

*These frameworks are evidence-informed organizing tools, not diagnostic instruments. Return to them when you need a simpler way to locate yourself inside a complicated pattern.*

**1. The Hope Loop**
`Uncertainty → Salience → Interpretation → Fantasy → Urge → Checking/Contact → Relief → Renewed Uncertainty`
*Use it to understand why short-term relief can keep long-term uncertainty active.*

**2. Reality–Story–Unknowns**
*   **Reality:** What was observed or explicitly said?
*   **Story:** What meaning have you added?
*   **Unknown:** What information is genuinely unavailable?
*Use it when interpretation starts feeling identical to fact.*

**3. The Five Displacements**
`Time · Attention · Attunement · Truth · Future`
*Use it to examine what has shifted in an existing relationship without reducing the question to whether one technical line was crossed.*

**4. Trigger-to-Need Map**
`Trigger → Interpretation → Body State → Urge → Behavior → Immediate Payoff → Longer-Term Cost → Need`
*Use it to find the actual places where a repetitive sequence can change.*

**5. Impact Before Story**
`What happened? → What did it cost? → What will change? → Then, what explains the pattern?`
*Use it when psychological explanation is in danger of arriving before accountability.*

**6. Distributed Need Fulfillment**
Spread important needs across a wider system of self, relationships, values, competence, community, pleasure, creativity, support, and meaningful activity.
*Use it when one person’s attention has begun carrying too much of your emotional life.*

### **Micro-Glossary**

*   **Cognitive Defusion:** Learning to experience thoughts as mental events rather than automatically treating them as commands, facts, or accurate descriptions of reality.
*   **Ego-dystonic:** Thoughts or behaviors that feel unwanted, inconsistent with the person’s values, or out of step with what they believe they truly want.
*   **Ego-syntonic:** Thoughts or behaviors that line up with what the person wants or believes about themselves.
*   **Enmeshment:** A relationship pattern in which the boundary between one person’s emotional life and another’s becomes difficult to maintain.
*   **Hyperactivating strategies:** Attachment behaviors involving turning the attachment system up (more attention, rumination, reassurance-seeking) under relational stress.
*   **Intermittent Reinforcement:** A behavioral principle where a behavior is rewarded sometimes rather than every time, leading to persistent behavior.
*   **Perceived Partner Responsiveness:** The feeling that a close partner understands you, values what matters to you, and cares about your well-being.
*   **Protest Behavior:** Indirect attempts to restore closeness or test importance (e.g., silence, criticism, creating tests) driven by attachment fear.
*   **Psychological Flexibility:** The ability to remain in contact with difficult thoughts and feelings while still behaving in ways that serve one’s values.

### **A Note on Evidence**

Limerence is a developing research area. The book therefore uses three kinds of knowledge deliberately rather than blending them together.

1.  **Direct limerence evidence** comes from studies that explicitly measure or investigate limerence. This literature is growing, but it remains much smaller than the literature available for established clinical and relationship constructs.
2.  **Adjacent evidence** comes from better-developed fields such as adult attachment, obsessive-compulsive behavior, rumination, romantic attraction, emotion regulation, grief, relationship science, behavioral learning, and psychological flexibility. These findings can illuminate plausible mechanisms without proving that the same mechanism causes limerence.
3.  **Author frameworks**—including the Hope Loop, Reality–Story–Unknowns, the Five Displacements, the Trigger-to-Need Map, Impact Before Story, and Distributed Need Fulfillment—are practical syntheses. They are meant to make complex material usable. They should not be mistaken for validated clinical tests or proprietary treatments.

Where the evidence is preliminary, correlational, indirect, or contested, the prose is intentionally conditional. Psychological explanations become less useful when they offer certainty the science has not earned.
