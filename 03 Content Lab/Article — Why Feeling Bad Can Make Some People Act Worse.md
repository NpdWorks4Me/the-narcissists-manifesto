# Why Feeling Bad Can Make Some People Act Worse

---

Let's start with something we all kind of assume: if you do something wrong and you feel awful about it, you stop doing it. That's how it works for most of us. Those feelings are supposed to be the brakes. They slow you down before you hurt someone again.

But some people? That awful feeling does the exact opposite. It doesn't slow them down. It makes them meaner. It makes them lash out *more*, not less.

I'm not here to excuse anyone. I'm not judging anybody. I just want you to know what you're looking at if you ever run into this. Because if you've been on the other side of it, you've probably blamed yourself. Let me help you not do that anymore.

---

## 1. The brakes work for most people. Not everyone.

Think of guilt and remorse like the brakes on a car. They're supposed to slow you down before you roll over someone you love.

For most of us, they do. We learn these feelings as kids, from our parents and the world around us. Over time they grow into our conscience. You know, that little inner voice that says "don't do that, that would hurt them." It's a good system. It keeps us kind.

But in certain people, the brakes are wired backwards. Instead of stopping the car, they hit the gas. So before we go any further, let's get the big words out of the way:

- **Guilt** = feeling bad about something you did.
- **Remorse** = feeling bad about hurting someone.
- **Conscience** = your inner "don't do that" voice.

Simple, right? Now here's the twist. For some people, those very feelings, the ones meant to protect the people around them, are what push them over the edge.

---

## 2. Two very different groups

Good news for your brain: not everyone with a hard personality is the same. There are two very different groups, and it's worth telling them apart.

**Group one. They feel the bad stuff, and it backfires.**
These are people with certain traits you might hear called borderline, or histrionic, or some anti-social patterns. Weirdly, they *do* feel guilt, shame, and remorse. But when they feel it, they don't get softer. They get angry. That "I'm a bad person" feeling is so unbearable that they lash out at whoever made them feel it. Usually that's the person closest to them.

**Group two. The bad stuff barely registers.**
These are people with strong narcissistic traits, or more serious anti-social behavior. Guilt? Hardly there. Remorse? Often missing entirely. They've built a wall of excuses so solid that they genuinely believe they're never wrong. It's not that they're quietly feeling bad inside. It's that the feeling doesn't get in at all.

Here's the sneaky part about the narcissist, though: they usually *do* feel shame. They just treat it like a raw nerve they'll do anything to protect. Guilt and remorse, though? Those are often just... not in the room.

So before you figure out what to do, figure out which group you're probably dealing with. It changes everything.

---

## 3. Reason one: they blame you instead of owning it

Here's how the backfire starts. A person does something hurtful. They feel bad about it. But that "feeling bad" is so uncomfortable, it makes them feel like *they're* the bad guy. Like they're not a good person anymore. And they can't stand that for even a second.

So they flip it. They point the finger at you.

"It's your fault."
"You made me do it."
"You guilt-tripped me."
"You triggered me."

This is called blaming the other person for your own feelings. The part that makes it so hard to handle is this: **you don't even have to have done anything wrong.** Sometimes just being there, just being the person they love, is enough for them to dump the blame on you. Your existence was the "reason."

And then they punish you for the feeling *they* created. The abuse doesn't stop. It escalates. Because in their head, you're the one who made them feel bad, so you deserve it.

If you hear "you're making me feel guilty" a lot, notice it. That's a big, loud tell.

---

## 4. Reason two: they turn feeling bad into their whole identity

This one's sneakier, because it sounds almost... noble. And it's not.

Instead of changing, some people hug the guilt close. They decide the bad feeling is just part of who they are. "This is my true nature." "That's just how I am." "I'm only being authentic."

So the behavior doesn't stop. It becomes a badge. They treat the guilt and shame like a price they're willing to pay. The "cost of being me." As long as they never have to change.

They'll even frame it as a kind of loyalty or honesty. "Take it or leave it, this is me." As if staying exactly the same, even when it hurts you, is proof of being real.

Here's the truth, friend to friend: **real change means owning up and then doing something different.** "That's just who I am" is usually a closed door pretending to be a deep answer. When someone uses it to keep hurting you, it's not authenticity. It's a reason to stop trying.

---

## 5. Reason three: they hand the bad feelings to you

This one leaves you feeling crazy, and I want you to know it's not in your head.

Some people can't stand carrying the "I'm the bad guy" feeling. So instead of dealing with it, they hand it to you. You end up feeling guilty. You end up feeling ashamed. You end up feeling small. Meanwhile, *they* get to act like the wounded one.

Ever walked away from an argument feeling like you did something wrong, even though *they* hurt *you*? That's not an accident. When someone pushes their bad feelings onto you, you don't win the argument. You just end up carrying their guilt.

And here comes the nasty part: they punish you for "making" them feel bad, on top of it. You get the blame *and* the guilt. It's a double hit.

If you're constantly the one apologizing, step back and ask a hard question. Who actually did what here? Because the pattern might be telling you more than the words.

---

## 6. The signs. A friendly checklist.

Let's pull it all together so you can actually use this. These don't mean a person is a monster. They mean: keep your eyes open, and protect yourself.

- They blame you for their own feelings ("you made me feel bad").
- They say "that's just who I am" when you ask for change.
- You're always the one apologizing.
- The bad feeling always seems to end up on *you*. You walk away feeling guilty, not them.
- When they hurt you and feel bad about it, they get *meaner*, not softer.

One more thing, to keep it fair: having boundaries isn't the same as being cruel. Needing space is normal. Wanting to feel safe is normal. This isn't about punishing people or labeling everyone around you. It's about seeing clearly so you don't drown in someone else's mess.

---

## 7. What this means for you

You can't fix a person who has made feeling bad into a weapon. And it's not your job to try.

But you *can* do a few things that might change a lot:

- Stop taking the blame they hand you.
- Stop apologizing for things you didn't do.
- Trust the pattern over the words. People tell you who they are in what they *do*, more than what they *say*.

Hold onto this one line, because it matters:

**Loving someone and letting them hurt you are not the same thing.** You're allowed to keep the love and walk away from the hurt. Both can be true at once. You don't have to pick between caring about them and protecting yourself.

---

## A last word

You're not crazy, and it's not in your head. When someone's bad feelings keep turning into *your* bad feelings, that's a real pattern. It's worth taking seriously.

This isn't a verdict on anyone. It's a warning, passed along like a friend would. So that if you ever see it, you recognize it for what it is, instead of blaming yourself.

Be kind, to them, if you can. And always, always to yourself.

You're allowed to step back and protect your peace. You don't owe your heart to anyone who turns your softness into a punching bag.

Stay safe out there.
