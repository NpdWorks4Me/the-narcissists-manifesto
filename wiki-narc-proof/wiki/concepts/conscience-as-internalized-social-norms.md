## Definition and formation

In the source transcript, **[[conscience]]** is described as an internal object that holds the socially transmitted instructions about how not to behave. Shame, guilt, and remorse are said to be, to a large extent, **social constructs**: they result from processes such as **[[socialization]]** and acculturation. Society’s edicts, conventions, mores, and norms are communicated through socialization agents such as parents, and these messages are **[[internalization|internalized]]** so that they become part of the person. That internalized content is what the source calls conscience. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

## Inhibitory function

Shame, guilt, and remorse are described as **inhibitory affects**: they inhibit action and act as "brakes" on misconduct. People who experience these emotions are therefore likely to cease and desist from abusive behavior. In this view, conscience ordinarily works against abuse rather than encouraging it. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

## Abuse as a paradoxical response

The transcript argues that shame, guilt, and remorse can nevertheless become drivers of abuse in certain individuals. This occurs when the person experiencing these emotions has high narcissistic defenses, antisocial or psychopathic traits, a "dark" personality style, or conditions such as **[[borderline personality disorder]]**. In such cases, the negative affects can trigger aggression and acting out rather than restraint. The source identifies three mechanisms that mediate the transition from shame, guilt, or remorse to abusive behavior: alloplastic defenses, affect matching, and reaction formation. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

### Alloplastic defenses

The first mechanism is **[[alloplastic defenses]]**. When a person with a dark personality or a cluster B personality disorder feels shame, guilt, or remorse, they may become angry at the perceived source of those feelings. They blame others — for example, the victim of their abuse — and may say "you made me feel this way" or "you made me do it." Because the other person is blamed for causing the discomfort, punishment and further abuse can follow. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

### Affect matching

The second mechanism is **affect matching**. Here, the person justifies their negative feelings by behaving in ways that correspond to them. They treat shame, guilt, and remorse as the "cost of being me" and refuse to change, framing change as self-betrayal. The emotions are accepted as consequences of an authentic self, and the behavior is therefore repeated rather than revised. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

### Reaction formation

The third mechanism is **[[reaction formation]]**. Because shame, guilt, and remorse are experienced as ego-dystonic — uncomfortable and unwanted — the person tries to get rid of them by inducing those same feelings in someone else. Through devaluation, **[[projection]]**, and **[[projective identification]]**, the abuser assigns their own unacceptable feelings to the victim: "they should feel guilty, ashamed, and remorseful, not I." This can become a fixed, identity-forming narrative. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

## Individual differences

The source also distinguishes between conditions. Narcissists are said to be capable of **[[shame]]** but rarely experience guilt and never remorse; psychopaths are described as incapable of shame. By contrast, people with borderline personality disorder, secondary psychopathy, and histrionic personality disorder can experience shame, guilt, and remorse. In narcissistic personality disorder — especially malignant narcissism — as well as antisocial personality disorder and primary psychopathy, the source claims there is no trace of guilt or remorse. Instead, defenses such as reframing, self-justification, and narrative construction protect the person from these conscience-related emotions. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]
