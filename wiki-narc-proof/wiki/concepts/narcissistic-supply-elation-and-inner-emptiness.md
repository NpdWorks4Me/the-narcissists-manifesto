# Narcissistic supply, elation, and inner emptiness

## Overview

The concepts of narcissistic supply, narcissistic elation, and inner emptiness appear together in discussions of narcissistic personality within the *Psychodynamic Diagnostic Manual* (PDM). According to the PDM’s object-relations view, the characteristic subjective experience of narcissistic individuals is a sense of inner emptiness and meaninglessness. This emptiness requires recurrent infusions of external affirmation, which the manual calls *narcissistic supply* — affirmation of the person’s importance and value. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

The PDM places narcissistic personalities on a continuum of severity, from neurotic to psychotic levels of organization. Toward the neurotic end, narcissistic individuals may be socially appropriate, personally successful, and charming, though somewhat deficient in intimacy. At more pathological levels, they may suffer from identity diffusion concealed by grandiose self-presentation, lack an inner-directed morality, and behave in ways that are destructive to others. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

## Inner emptiness and narcissistic supply

The PDM states that narcissistic individuals experience inner emptiness and meaninglessness that must be filled by external affirmation. This view has roots in object relations theory; in classical psychoanalysis, the so-called “empty schizoid core” was first identified with borderline personality, and only later came to be associated with narcissism through the work of scholars such as Guntrip and Seinfeld. The PDM adopts this object-relations understanding. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

Narcissistic supply may take the form of status, admiration, wealth, or success. When narcissistic individuals succeed in extracting such affirmation, they may feel an internal elation — referred to as *narcissistic elation*. In this state they behave in a grandiose and arrogant manner, with a sense of entitlement, and treat people perceived as lower status with contempt. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

## Narcissistic collapse and covert presentation

When the environment fails to provide ongoing narcissistic supply, the PDM describes a state known as *narcissistic collapse*. In collapse, narcissistic individuals may feel depressed, ashamed, and envious of those who attain the status they lack. They may fantasize about unlimited success, beauty, glory, and power, and their lack of real pleasure in work or love can be painful to witness. This applies equally to successful narcissists. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

According to the source, every narcissist cycles through overt and covert phases, but narcissists tend to present to therapy only in the covert phase. This can create the false impression that there are two types of narcissists — overt and covert — when in fact it is the same narcissist experiencing collapse. Both phases involve grandiosity; covert narcissists are internally preoccupied with grandiose fantasies even when they appear shy, ashamed, and diffident. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

## Related clinical features

The PDM notes that narcissistic individuals frequently have hypochondriacal preoccupations and somatic complaints. They spend considerable energy evaluating their status relative to others and defend their wounded self-esteem through idealizing and devaluing others. When they idealize someone, they feel more special or important by association; when they devalue someone, they feel superior. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

Attachment and mentalization literature suggests that narcissistic people often have insecure attachment styles, including both attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance. This may result from early relationships that were confusing, unpredictable, and full of hidden agendas. In such circumstances, children may respond by divesting themselves of meaningful emotional investment in others and becoming preoccupied with themselves and their bodily integrity. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]

## Impact on therapists

Therapists working with narcissistic patients may feel unreasonably idealized, unreasonably devalued, or simply disregarded. Effects can include boredom, detachment, distraction, daydreaming, inability to focus attention, mild irritation, impatience, and feeling invisible. A narcissistic patient, especially one of the grandiose overt type, may be experienced as speaking *at* rather than *to* the therapist. ^[yt-nmb4uke-vva.md:9-9]
