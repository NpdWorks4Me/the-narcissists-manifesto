# Persecutory Object (Persary) in Narcissism

The **persecutory object**, rendered in some sources as **persary** or **per-secondary object**, is a term used in Sam Vaknin’s account of pathological narcissism. It refers to a devalued object — typically an intimate partner or caregiver figure — that the narcissist comes to perceive as an internal enemy. Where the same object was once idealized, it is later experienced as hostile, corrosive, and dangerous to the narcissist’s inner world. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Place in the narcissistic shared fantasy

The narcissist’s relational life is organized around a Shared Fantasy, a shared internal narrative that requires at least one participant to sustain. Early in the relationship the narcissist idealizes the partner, treating them as a Maternal Figure and investing them with unconditional, loving qualities. During this phase, the narcissist fears abandonment by this idealized object. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

Later, the shared fantasy moves into devaluation and discard. The formerly idealized object is “transmogrified” into a **persary**: an object immersed in enmity and hostility. The narcissist then experiences the devalued object as an inner persecutor, linked to the rejecting or sadistic mother-image. In this phase, abandonment is no longer feared but actively wished for, because it is experienced as a purging or cleansing of the narcissist’s internal space. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Annihilation anxiety and twin anxieties

The persecutory object is central to one of the narcissist’s “twin anxieties.” During idealization, the narcissist shows abandonment anxiety; during devaluation, the narcissist develops a dread of annihilation by the persecutory object. The devalued object is perceived as an immortal enemy intent on dissolving or destroying the narcissist. Thus the narcissist craves rejection and abandonment by this object, since removal of the object helps relieve the threat. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

In Vaknin’s framework, these twin anxieties do not drive narcissistic behavior. Rather, the narcissist’s behavior is dictated by the shared-fantasy algorithm, and the anxieties arise as consequences of that process. This is contrasted with Borderline Personality Disorder, where twin anxieties are more directly causal of approach-avoidance behavior. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Relational implications

According to the source, the shift from idealized to persecutory object determines whether abandonment can be used as leverage. During the idealization phase, a credible threat of abandonment may rapidly modify the narcissist’s behavior. During the devaluation phase, however, the same threat backfires: it confirms the narcissist’s view of the partner as a persecutory enemy, validates the narcissist’s aggression, and may make him more defiant and energized. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]
