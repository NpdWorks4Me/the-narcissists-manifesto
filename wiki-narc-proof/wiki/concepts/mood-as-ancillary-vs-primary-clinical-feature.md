## Mood as ancillary vs. primary clinical feature

The distinction between mood as an **ancillary** feature and mood as a **primary** clinical feature is used clinically to separate borderline personality disorder from bipolar disorder. In bipolar disorder, mood cycling is the central mechanism: cycles take a long time, typically weeks to months, and move between manic, hypomanic, and depressive phases. Because the mood disturbance is fundamental, everything experienced by the individual is described as a direct outcome and result of the underlying mood disorder. ^[yt-fzra3-s4bak.md:9]

In borderline personality disorder, by contrast, mood is **ancillary** rather than primary. The mood cycling is much faster — a person can switch moods within the hour or within hours, and a mood lasting four days is considered an outlier. Other clinical features are said to be much more relevant in borderline personality disorder and are completely absent in bipolar disorder. The source is blunt that anyone who confuses the two conditions is a bad clinician. ^[yt-fzra3-s4bak.md:9]

The ancillary role of mood in borderline personality disorder has important relational consequences. Bipolar cycling is described as predictable: a child can anticipate that a manic phase will be followed by withdrawal and depression. With a borderline mother, no model or theory can predict future behavior, producing a failure of theory of mind and mentalization. Because the child cannot construct a workable theory of the mother's mind, the child cannot feel that they are in a secure base, which is described as terrifying. ^[yt-fzra3-s4bak.md:9]

The same presentation includes a "bad object" message that the child is unworthy, a failure, or disappointing, and the mother sets the child up for failure. A child confronted with a dysfunctional mother usually cannot conclude that the mother is the problem, because that would threaten survival; instead, the child concludes "I am the problem" and grows up feeling like wrongness personified. This can make later self-awareness difficult, because it requires admitting that nothing is wrong with oneself and that the problem was the mother — an admission the inner child still experiences as life-threateningly unsafe. ^[yt-fzra3-s4bak.md:9]

The source transcript also offers an anecdotal illustration: a mother with borderline personality disorder related well to babies and young children but became conflictual when children developed their own thoughts and defied her. Her condition was attributed to early maternal loss, abandonment, trauma before age twenty, and lack of help, while the family refrain was "that's just how she is." ^[yt-fzra3-s4bak.md:9]
