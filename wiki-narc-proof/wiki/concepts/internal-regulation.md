# Internal Regulation

Internal regulation is the process by which a person stabilizes their own moods and emotions from within, without relying on other people. The source describes it as the characteristic mode of healthy, normal people, who have internal regulatory mechanisms and are self-sufficient in reaching a state of equilibrium and homeostasis. Such people do not need the presence or contributions of others to regulate their internal states; they are stable around the average, without extreme highs or lows, and their emotions function as signals rather than threats.^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

The concept is chiefly defined in contrast to [[External Regulation]], which is the use of other people to regulate and stabilize one's own emotions and moods. [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] involves an [[External Locus of Control]]: the person's life is determined externally rather than internally, and they are controlled from the outside. The source notes that this can feel egodistonic and even menacing, yet some vulnerable people prefer [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] even when internal regulation is available, such as in the wake of [[Psychotherapy]]. There is strong resistance to internal regulation in people with [[Borderline Personality Disorder]] and [[Narcissistic Personality Disorder]].^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

One explanation for this resistance is that [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] offers advantages. It supports [[Alloplastic Defenses]], allowing the person to shift blame onto others and avoid the negative effects of shame and guilt. It is also described as anxiolytic, because someone who is regulated externally is not held accountable for adverse outcomes. These benefits help explain why internal regulation may be rejected despite being more self-sufficient and healthy.^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

The source treats internal regulation as the normative baseline against which [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] — and its pathological forms — can be understood. In healthy relationships, some exchange of agency and dependency may occur and be called [[Intimacy]]; at extremes, dependence can devolve into coercion and fantasy. Internal regulation, by contrast, means retaining one's own capacity for emotional stability without outsourcing it to another person or group.^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]
