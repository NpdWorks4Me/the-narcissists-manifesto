# Borderline Personality Disorder as a Subspecies of Dissociative Identity Disorder

## Overview

According to a source transcript by Sam Batn, borderline personality disorder (BPD) “can best be described as a subspecies of dissociative identity disorder” ([[Dissociative Identity Disorder|DID]]). On this view, what clinicians call mood lability and emotional dysregulation in BPD are actually the outward manifestations of switching between self-states, comparable to the shift from a host personality to an alter personality in DID. The source also links BPD to [[Secondary Psychopathy]] and [[Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder|CPTSD]]. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

## Shared features with secondary psychopathy and CPTSD

The transcript argues that borderline and histrionic personality disorders may, in women, be manifestations of secondary-type psychopathy, a variant of antisocial personality disorder measured by Factor 2 of the standard PCL-R. Survivors of CPTSD are said to show a “psychopathic and narcissistic overlay,” but in survivors these behaviors are transient, whereas in borderline women they are not. Listed shared features include impulsivity, defiant grandiosity, antisocial and interpersonal aggression, manipulativeness, emotional dysregulation, paranoia, lack of object constancy, attachment dysfunction, hostility, splitting, distress, anxiety, depression, and substance abuse. The source further claims that a majority of BPD diagnoses are women, and that these women tend to defy gender roles and emulate psychopathic men. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

## Dissociative mechanisms and triggers

Dissociation, depersonalization, and derealization are described as common reactions in BPD, but also in DID, PTSD, and CPTSD. In the source’s account, when a borderline woman experiences intolerable inner dissonance—for example guilt, shame, pain, or anticipated abandonment—she “hands over control” to an inner secondary psychopath and begins to dissociate. These dissociative states are described as being “on autopilot,” sliding into anesthesia, or becoming a spectator. The psychodynamic aim is said to be distancing from events, pain, and anticipated rejection, as if saying “this is not happening to me.” Substance abuse and addictive distractions such as bar hopping or video games are said to exacerbate these reactive patterns, and patients may misattribute subsequent amnesia to alcohol or drugs rather than to the emergence of another self-state. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

The source states that the dissociative trigger in BPD is typically actual abandonment or the perceived threat of rejection from an intimate partner, producing unbearable separation anxiety. The borderline patient is said to generate the very situations she fears: afraid of abandonment, she abandons; afraid of abuse, she abuses. Severe dissociation is noted to be diagnostic criterion number nine for BPD. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

## The psychopathic alter and differential behavior

When one of the borderline patient’s alters is psychopathic, the patient may be antisocial, impulsive, disempathic, lying, aggressive, defiant, and capable of remaining alone for long periods, like a “lone wolf in her lair.” By contrast, when the alter is grandiose or narcissistic, the patient tends to seek company and cannot tolerate even one day without an intimate partner, feeling dead inside unless loved. The transcript also claims it is a myth that BPD or DID patients cannot control the behaviors of their alters, noting that courts worldwide reject these diagnoses as a mitigating circumstance or defense. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

## Decompensation, acting out, and psychosis

The source cites a 1979 clinical note in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*—attributed to Kavanaar, Sullivan, and Malpi, later given as Coor, Sullivan, and Maltby—regarding narcissistic and histrionic people, mostly women, who react to unwanted sexual advances or unreciprocated sexual interest with a transient form of psychosis. Attributions include oral narcissistic structures and immature object relations. The source adds cases in which a woman succeeds in having sex with the desired man but is then emotionally abandoned or ignored. In all these cases, defense mechanisms are said to switch off, producing acting out such as unprotected sex, binge drinking, reckless driving, or shopping sprees, behaviors the source describes as secondary psychopathy. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]
