# Borderline Personality Disorder as an Invisibility Cloak

**Borderline Personality Disorder as an Invisibility Cloak** is a metaphor used in a clinical discussion of personality disorders to describe how borderline traits can mask more entrenched conditions underneath. In this view, the borderline presentation is not necessarily the deepest layer of pathology: when it recedes, the underlying narcissistic, histrionic, or psychopathic features become visible. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## The Invisibility Cloak Metaphor

The speaker likens [[Borderline Personality Disorder]] to an invisibility cloak. Once the cloak is "tossed away," what remains is the "Wizard of Oz behind the curtain" — the individual's true face, which may include narcissism, [[Histrionic Personality Disorder]], or [[Psychopathy]]. The implication is that borderline features may soften or mitigate over time while co-occurring disorders persist or become more apparent. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Aging and Intensification

When a mother's difficult behavior solidifies or intensifies with age, the source suggests she is "very likely not a borderline, or at least not a pure borderline." Instead, she is likely to have co-morbidities that worsen over time, especially [[Narcissistic Personality Disorder]]. Unlike some other traits, narcissism generally does not improve with age; the exception is antisocial behaviors. In cases involving [[Dementia]], certain [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]] can become more pronounced, and narcissistic features may become "much more deleterious and egregious." The borderline part may be reduced, while narcissism, psychopathy, and histrionic features survive. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Psychopathy as a Co-Morbidity

The discussion addresses psychopathy as existing on a spectrum. The "overwhelming vast majority" of psychopaths are not violent criminals or serial killers; many are functional and socially adaptive. Studies cited in the source suggest that psychopaths are more prevalent among chief executive officers and surgeons than in the general population. The key differences from a healthy person are goal orientation, lack of empathy, and tendencies toward being callous, reckless, unscrupulous, and immoral. The source notes there is debate over whether [[Psychopathy]] should be considered a mental illness at all. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

For borderline personality disorder, the co-morbidity with [[Antisocial Personality Disorder]] — described as the clinical name for psychopathy — is "pretty high," with "learned guesses" that about one in three borderline individuals may also be psychopathic. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Superficial Charm and the Child's Experience

A key clinical feature of psychopathy is superficial charm and glibness, which can make a parent appear charismatic and magnetic in public. The child may see the mother interface with the world warmly while experiencing cruelty behind closed doors, leading to confusion and disbelief. The main message such a mother conveys to the child is an underlying accusation: "something's wrong with you," "you are constantly failing me," or "you are a disappointment." ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

Roles such as [[Scapegoat]] and [[Golden Child]] may be transitory. The child can never win: when the child gratifies the mother, she may feel suffocated or threatened by the expectation to reciprocate, and may suddenly flip, rendering the child an enemy. This leads to what the source calls an "approach-avoidance repetition compulsion" and an inability to reach a plateau of intimacy. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Lasting Internal Effects

The child may develop what is called an "internalized bad object" — a constellation of inner voices telling the child they are unworthy, inadequate, a failure, ugly, stupid, or disappointing. These voices are known as [[Introjects]] and can persist for life, corroding self-esteem even after the child has become accomplished or believes they have recovered. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]
