# Narcissistic Abuse as an Idealization Test

**Narcissistic abuse as an idealization test** is a concept articulated by Sam Vaknin, describing one of the deeper functions of abuse within relationships involving a narcissist. Vaknin argues that narcissistic abuse is an inevitable feature of the narcissist's interpersonal relationships, couched as it is within the shared fantasy. While abuse is often discussed as a means of devaluation, it can also function as a way of *testing* whether a partner possesses the idealized qualities the narcissist attributes to them. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

Narcissistic abuse fulfills multiple functions simultaneously. It tests people's unconditional love and object constancy — whether they would forgive transgressions and remain in the relationship. It also serves to devalue people, either to discard them or to make the narcissist feel superior to them. These functions, Vaknin explains, cater to so many psychological needs that the abuse is very hard to eradicate or reverse. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

## The Role of Narcissistic Supply

The narcissist is addicted to narcissistic supply — attention, adulation, and other forms of feedback that regulate his or her emotional states. In the absence of supply, the narcissist becomes disregulated and may even become suicidal in the wake of narcissistic mortification. For the narcissist, supply is not simply a matter of vanity or triumph; it is a matter of survival. The relationship between narcissistic supply and the narcissist is like the relationship between water and a plant: without it, the narcissist shrivels and withers. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

To obtain supply, the narcissist must solicit it from other people, even though he or she holds everyone in contempt. This forced dependence is deeply humiliating and creates a cognitive and emotional dissonance: the narcissist sees himself as godlike and superior, yet must grovel for attention. This ineluctable dependency amounts to a massive narcissistic injury, generating resentment and rage that later crystallize into an organizing worldview of cosmic injustice. The narcissist abuses his or her sources of supply partly to restore a sense of balance and to buttress the shattered fantastic sense of superiority. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

## Abuse as a Test of Idealized Qualities

Counterintuitively, narcissistic abuse can assist in *idealizing* the intimate partner. When the narcissist idealizes another person, the narcissist is actually idealizing himself or herself through a process known as **co-idealization**. If the partner is extraordinary, beautiful, intelligent, or unique, then the narcissist, who "owns" the partner, becomes equally extraordinary by association. Idealization thus elevates the narcissist's own grandiosity, much like owning a luxury car functions as a status symbol. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

Narcissistic abuse is, in this light, a test of the partner's strength, cohesion, resiliency, dedication, commitment, faithfulness, and capacity to love. If the partner survives the abuse and remains in the relationship despite the purgatory they are subjected to, it proves that the partner is strong and resilient — "proves her metal." These are idealized dimensions of the partner, and only abuse can expose whether they are imaginary or real. Narcissistic abuse therefore renders the partner unique, inordinately resilient, unusually strong, and capable of loving, making the partner worthy of the narcissist's equally godlike attributes. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

## Relationship to Borderline Dynamics

Vaknin situates pathological narcissism within what Otto Kernberg called the borderline personality organization. The narcissist shares with the borderline a dependence on the partner as an external regulator of moods and emotions. However, whereas the borderline reacts with approach-avoidance repetition compulsion and clinging dependency, the narcissist resents the dependency and is likely to react psychopathically or antisocially — abusing, tormenting, and seeking to dismantle the partner. This dynamic also manifests as intermittent reinforcement: hot and cold, "I love you, I hate you," idealization followed by devaluation. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]

## Practical Implications

Classifying narcissistic abuse by its function has practical, therapeutic, and behavioral implications. Vaknin suggests that when the narcissist abuses you, you can ask yourself whether the abuse is testing your unconditional love, idealizing you by proving your strength and capacity to love, devaluing you, or expressing anger about dependency. Identifying the function may allow for coping strategies — for example, signaling information to the narcissist that could ameliorate and mitigate the virulence of the attack. ^[yt-drlgalrra9k.md:9]
