## Pathological [[external-regulation|External Regulation]]

**External regulation** is the use of other people to regulate or stabilize one’s own emotions and moods. When a person is overwhelmed by emotions or experiences labile, fluctuating moods, they may turn to others to induce inner peace, harmony, and stability. This stands in contrast to healthy self-regulation, in which people manage their emotional states internally and remain relatively stable around an average, treating emotions as signals rather than threats. Emotion dysregulation and mood liability are key clinical features of conditions such as borderline personality disorder. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

### Why [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] Is Appealing

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is associated with an external locus of control, dependence on others, and a sense of being controlled from the outside — a feeling that is egodistonic and even menacing. Nevertheless, it offers tradeoffs that make it addictive. Because the external regulator is blamed for failures, wrong choices, and adverse outcomes, the regulated person can avoid shame, guilt, remorse, and regret through alloplastic defenses. It is also anxiolytic: when someone else is in charge of one’s emotions and moods, the individual is absolved of responsibility and accountability. The source compares this dynamic to the Faustian deal offered by dictators and authoritarian leaders, who ask the populace to surrender power in exchange for freedom from anxiety, guilt, and shame. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

### Group and Narrative Dimensions

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is common in collective settings. In groups, the group itself regulates the individual: it controls behaviors, cognitions, emotions, choices, and decisions, producing a cult mind or mob mind. Peer pressure is another form of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]], in which peers communicate expectations and punish nonconformity. These processes are mediated by narratives and symbols, including religion, morality plays, conspiracy theories, and other symbolic frameworks. The source invokes Lacan’s distinction between the imaginary and the symbolic to describe how narratives carry out [[external-regulation|External Regulation]], reduce anxiety, and absolve the individual from responsibility, thereby enabling integration into a group. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

### Intimacy and Regulatory Outsourcing

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is described as a precondition for intimacy, since intimacy involves both the communication of vulnerability and the handing over of power or control to another person. The source generalizes this as “regulatory outsourcing.” In healthy relationships, a degree of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is normal; when it enhances well-being and allows partners to maintain separate lives, it is called intimacy. When taken to an extreme, however, it becomes pathology — for example, in borderline personality disorder and dependent personality disorder, colloquially known as codependence. In intimate relationships, the partner becomes the “outsourced and externalized ego”: for the borderline, the partner is expected to regulate emotions, moods, needs, and expectations; for the codependent, the partner is expected to regulate the narcissist’s self-concept through narcissistic supply. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

### Pathological Forms

Narcissistic personality disorder involves an incapacity for true intimacy. Instead, narcissists replace it with fantastic intimacy — an intimacy lived in fantasy or shared fantasy, through which they become regulated externally via narcissistic supply. In all relationships, [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] can devolve from healthy interdependence into dependence, and eventually into ugly forms of coercion, fantasy, and worse. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]
