# Narcissist Twin Anxieties

**Narcissist twin anxieties** is a concept discussed by Sam Vaknin in a video on managing narcissistic abuse. It describes two distinct anxiety states experienced by a narcissist: **abandonment anxiety** during the idealization phase and **annihilation anxiety** during the devaluation and discard phases. The concept is used to explain why a narcissist who fears abandonment nevertheless pushes people away. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Background

Vaknin traces the narcissist’s abandonment anxiety to childhood relationships with the maternal figure — not necessarily the biological mother, but the caregiver who played a maternal role. The maternal figure prevents the child from separating and becoming an individual, the child retreats into fantasy, and the result is a terror of abandonment. This fear is a clinical feature shared with borderline personality organization: both narcissists and borderlines dread abandonment and rejection. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

In the narcissist, however, abandonment anxiety is not focused on a specific person leaving. It is a general ambient fear of being left alone, because when alone the narcissist cannot maintain the **shared fantasy** — the narcissist’s “living space.” The shared fantasy must be shared by a participant to provide [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] such as narcissistic supply and some [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]]. Without it, the narcissist collapses. Abandonment therefore resembles a catastrophic failure, sits on the border between narcissistic injury and narcissistic mortification, and is a major affective feature of narcissistic collapse. It reminds the narcissist how fragile and delusional his world is. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## The twin anxieties

The apparent contradiction between fear of abandonment and devaluing others dissolves when the phases of the shared fantasy are considered. Initially, the narcissist idealizes an intimate partner, best friend, or co-worker, converting them into a maternal figure. During this idealization phase, the narcissist becomes attached and is terrified of abandonment by the idealized object. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

Later, the narcissist’s behavior is dictated by an unstoppable, immutable algorithm — the shared fantasy — leading to devaluation and discard. At that point, the internal object is transformed from an idealized, unconditionally loving object into a **persecutory object**: an enemy, or the sadistic, harsh inner mother. The narcissist now craves abandonment by this devalued object, perceiving it as a purging or cleansing of internal space. Thus, the narcissist dreads abandonment by the idealized object while wishing for abandonment by the devalued object. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Difference from borderline personality disorder

Twin anxieties are typical of [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]]. Borderlines also experience twin anxieties: abandonment anxiety and [[engulfment-anxiety|Engulfment Anxiety]] when a partner becomes too intimate, producing an approach-avoidance repetition compulsion. In borderline personality disorder, these anxieties drive the behavior and are its cause. In pathological narcissism, by contrast, the behaviors are dictated by the shared-fantasy algorithm, and the twin anxieties are brought on by those behaviors rather than being their cause. Vaknin presents this as a major psychodynamic difference between unadulterated narcissism and borderline personality organization. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

## Leveraging the anxiety in a relationship

Vaknin’s primary advice is **no contact**: cut losses and get away. But if that is not possible, emotionally or financially, he suggests making use of the narcissist’s twin anxieties. During the idealization phase, abandonment can be used as a tool to modify the narcissist’s behavior. By communicating verbally, through body language, or behaviorally that a boundary breach or transgression will result in abandonment, the partner can strike compromises, establish boundaries, and level the playing field. Vaknin describes this as a form of blackmail, and says even the mere mention of abandonment can quickly transform the narcissist’s conduct. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]

This tool is not available during the devaluation phase. Threatening to abandon a narcissist at that stage would make him happy and euphoric, because it confirms the narcissist’s view of the partner as an enemy or persecutory object. It would validate him, energize him, and make him more defiant, reckless, aggressive, and demanding. ^[yt-82nfsr9n2xg.md:9]
