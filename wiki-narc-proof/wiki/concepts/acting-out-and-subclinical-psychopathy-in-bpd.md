## Acting Out and Subclinical Psychopathy in BPD

In material on borderline personality disorder, acting out is described as closely tied to subclinical psychopathy. The borderline personality organization is presented as a confluence of painful internal processes, coupled in many cases with subclinical psychopathy, specifically Factor 2 psychopathy. Acting out is said to be essentially psychopathic: when a person with BPD acts out, the partner is no longer in her mind, and a psychopathic self-state takes over. The individual is described as "out of her mind" and no longer herself at that moment. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

### Acting Out

Acting out can take many forms, most of them reckless. Examples given in the source include promiscuity and sex with strangers or relative strangers, shopaholism that depletes family finances, workaholism, and substance abuse and addictions. The behavior is understood as a way of protecting the self from overwhelming, labile, and all-consuming emotions and moods. In this state, the person may appear heartless and indifferent to her partner because the partner is no longer mentally represented; "out of sight, out of mind" prevails. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

### Underlying Processes

The source identifies a set of psychodynamic processes that motivate acting out and avoidant behavior in BPD. Although it says there are five such processes, the transcript details four of them.

**Object inconstancy.** The first process is introject or object inconstancy: the inability to maintain internal representations, objects, or "snapshots" of significant others. The person cannot trust the permanence of meaningful others in her life, which provokes abandonment anxiety or separation insecurity. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

**Transitional or comfort objectifying.** The second process is a failure to attach, bond, or cathect emotionally to other people. The person with BPD uses others' bodies, presence, minds, love, and emotions the way small children use teddy bears or favorite blankets — as comfort objects or transitional objects. The source describes this as being stuck in the limbo between narcissistic libido and other-directed libido. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

**Identity disturbance.** The third process is identity disturbance: fluctuating between mutually exclusive and incompatible beliefs, values, behavior patterns, cognitions, and emotions, owing to the absence of a core identity. The source describes the borderline as "the reification of absence," similar to the narcissist, and notes that many scholars regard narcissism and BPD as essentially the same condition with an empty schizoid core. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

**Pervasive dissociation.** The fourth process is pervasive dissociation, involving bouts of amnesia, derealization, and depersonalization. The person may feel empty and unreal when alone, and may have a very low tolerance for loneliness. During acting-out sex, for example, the person may feel as if she is observing her own body rather than inhabiting it ("I'm not there"), may feel that events are dream-like or unreal, or may later forget that the event happened. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]

### Suggested Responses

The source also offers a set of repeated statements intended to address the twin anxieties said to fuel BPD behavior: abandonment anxiety and [[engulfment-anxiety|Engulfment Anxiety]]. These statements include: "I'm always here for you"; "You are and will always be dear to me"; "I do place boundaries, but also in order to be strong enough for both of us"; and "I will accept and respect any decision you make." The source suggests these sentences can help reduce abandonment anxiety without provoking [[engulfment-anxiety|Engulfment Anxiety]]. ^[yt-q1kj8o1cc-u.md:9]
