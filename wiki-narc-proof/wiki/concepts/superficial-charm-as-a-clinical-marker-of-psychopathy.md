# Superficial Charm as a Clinical Marker of Psychopathy

Superficial charm and glibness are described as key diagnostic and clinical features of [[psychopathy]]. In a clinical discussion, a person who is extremely charismatic and magnetic in public but cruel and hostile behind closed doors is said to be showing psychopathy rather than borderline personality disorder; the clinician states plainly, “you’re describing a psychopath, not a borderline.” Charm also appears in [[narcissistic personality disorder]], but “to a much lesser extent”; the antisocial dimension of narcissism is more specifically psychopathic. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Psychopathy as a spectrum

The overwhelming majority of psychopaths are not serial killers or hardened criminals. According to the source, most psychopaths are functional, pro-social, and socially adaptive, and may even be “pillars of the community.” Research cited in the transcript reported a higher prevalence of psychopathy among chief executive officers and surgeons than in the general population. The key differences from healthy persons are that psychopaths tend to be goal-oriented, lack empathy, and are therefore callous, reckless, unscrupulous, and immoral. These observations have prompted debate about whether [[psychopathy]] should be considered a mental illness at all. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Relationship to borderline personality disorder

The source describes borderline personality disorder as an “invisibility cloak” that can conceal other personality traits. When borderline features soften or are shed, what remains may include [[narcissistic personality disorder]], [[histrionic personality disorder]], and psychopathy. With age, narcissistic features worsen — with the exception of antisocial behaviors — and similar accentuation can occur in dementia. Comorbidity between borderline personality disorder and [[antisocial personality disorder]], which the source calls the clinical name for psychopathy, is high; “learned guesses” suggest about one in three people with borderline personality disorder may also be psychopathic. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Diagnostic and interpersonal consequences

The superficial charm of a psychopathic mother can be confusing to a child, because outsiders experience her as lovely, charismatic, and magnetic while the child experiences the opposite behind closed doors. The clinician notes that this pattern conveys an underlying message to the child: “something is wrong with you,” and that the child is failing or disappointing. In this account, external charm is not merely social skill but a clinical marker that helps distinguish psychopathy from borderline personality disorder. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]
