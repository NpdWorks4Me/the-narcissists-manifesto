## Communication Protocols in Relationships

A **communication protocol** in this context is a rigid, strict set of rules that governs how partners express themselves to each other, particularly when one partner experiences intense emotions. In the source material, such protocols are recommended for relationships involving a person described as having borderline traits. They are described as "very powerful tools" that can prevent misunderstanding and pain later in the relationship. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

### Core rules

The protocol begins with prohibitions: no personal attacks, and no attacking the other person. Instead of accusing a partner with statements like "the way you are misbehaving is really bad," the speaker should describe their own internal state. A modeled example is: "I'm in pain." The instruction is to talk about oneself and one's own reactions rather than about the other person. The source advises that partners should adhere to such protocols "religiously." ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

### Supporting techniques

Communication protocols are part of a broader set of techniques for helping a partner externalize and verbalize emotions. One method mentioned is **chair work**, in which the person places an emotion — anger, envy, fear — on an empty chair and dialogues with it, or interrogates and asks it for help. Another method is cognitive behavioral therapy, used to counter negative automatic thoughts that lead to catastrophizing, despair, depression, and anxiety. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

For anger, the source recommends anger management techniques, especially **cognitive restructuring**. This involves teaching the person to think about triggers, stimuli, provocations, fears, and frustrations in a completely different way — for example, as opportunities for growth and learning. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

**Humor** is described as "the best antidote to anger." The source cautions against mocking, invalidating, or minimizing the angry person's feelings. Instead, the partner should say something humorous that exposes the situation as irrational, which can diffuse anger if the person is amenable to humor. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

### Mood lability and structure

The source describes **mood lability** — rapid and severe mood swings — as a serious problem for someone with borderline traits, with mood states shifting within hours. Because these moods are largely reactive rather than internally generated, the recommended countermeasures include physical activity, a sleep schedule, rigid routines, and stress management techniques. The source also notes that reality testing may be impaired, so eliminating environmental triggers and structuring life to reduce stress is important: stress leads to anxiety, anxiety leads to mood lability, and reducing stress can interrupt the whole chain reaction. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

### Locus of control and responsibility

The source argues that a partner with borderline traits may "outsource" internal ego functions to the intimate partner, making the partner the "source of regulation" and transferring her locus of control. This is described as unhealthy because it is counterfactual and amounts to scapegoating the partner. The recommended approach is to help her regain an internal locus of control gradually and lovingly, without threats, demands, or criticism. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

The source also distinguishes between **autoplastic defenses** — accepting responsibility for one's actions and consequences — and **alloplastic defenses**, which blame other people for everything. Regaining control includes shifting language: instead of saying "this happened to me," the person is encouraged to say "I did it." The partner is advised to reward responsible behavior, refuse to accept responsibility for the other person's actions, moods, or aggression, and do so lovingly. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]

### Idealization and devaluation

The source notes that, like the narcissist, a person with borderline traits tends to idealize and devalue partners, but in very rapid cycles — sometimes several times a day. When idealizing, the partner is seen as a god; when devaluing, as the devil, and the person may feel little regret about betraying or harming the partner during devaluation. These dynamics are presented as background context for why strict communication protocols and emotional regulation techniques are necessary. ^[yt-l7xhxkpxx0e.md:9]
