# External Regulation

**External regulation** is the use of other people to regulate or stabilize one’s own emotions and moods. When a person feels overwhelmed by emotions or experiences unstable moods, they may turn to others to restore inner peace and harmony. This contrasts with [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]], where healthy individuals use their own psychological mechanisms to stabilize fluctuating moods without depending on others. Healthy people are generally stable around an average, and their emotions function as signals rather than threats. External regulation becomes especially relevant in conditions such as [[borderline personality disorder]], where emotion dysregulation and mood liability are key features. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## Why People Choose External Regulation

External regulation is often associated with an external [[locus of control]]: the person feels controlled from the outside, not in charge of their own life. This can feel egodystonic and even menacing. Yet vulnerable individuals may still prefer it because it offers tradeoffs. One advantage is the use of alloplastic defenses: if things go wrong, the person can blame others and avoid shame and guilt. Another is anxiety reduction — external regulation is anxiolytic, since someone else is responsible for one’s emotions and actions. The absence of accountability can feel addictive. The source compares this to populist or authoritarian bargains, where a leader says, "It's all my responsibility" in exchange for the populace giving up power and escaping anxiety, guilt, and shame. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## Social and Collective Dynamics

External regulation is common in collective settings. In groups, the group itself may regulate the individual: members assimilate into the collective and develop a "cult mind" or "mob mind." The group then controls behaviors, cognitions, emotions, choices, and decisions, becoming the locus of control. Similar dynamics appear in peer interactions; [[peer pressure]] is a form of external regulation in which peers communicate expectations and punish nonconformity. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## Narratives and Symbolic Regulation

External regulation is mediated through narratives and symbols. Morality plays, conspiracy theories, and religion can all serve as vehicles of external control: in religion, the person is controlled by God; in a morality play, by the force of good against evil. Manipulating symbols can involve relinquishing control and relegating it to a symbolic realm, a process related to [[Lacan]]'s concepts of the imaginary and the symbolic. These narratives reduce anxiety, absolve the individual of responsibility, and allow integration into a group. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## External Regulation and Intimacy

External regulation may be a precondition for [[intimacy]]. Intimacy involves both the communication of vulnerability and handing over some power or control to another person. It can be understood as a form of regulatory outsourcing. People with [[narcissistic personality disorder]] are said to be incapable of this kind of intimacy; instead, they replace it with fantastic intimacy — an entirely internal fantasy. When the narcissist becomes embedded in a shared fantasy or "paracosm," they can become regulated from the outside through narcissistic supply and the shared fantasy itself. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## Pathological Manifestations

When external regulation becomes intense and all-pervasive, it appears as pathology. The source mentions borderline personality disorder and dependent personality disorder (colloquially "codependence"). In interpersonal relationships, the intimate partner is expected to regulate the person’s happiness, well-being, moods, affections, and emotions — effectively becoming an outsourced, externalized ego. The specific expectation varies: in borderline personality disorder, the partner is expected to regulate needs and expectations; in codependence, the partner is expected to regulate the narcissist’s self-concept through self-enhancement or [[narcissistic supply]]. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]

## Healthy vs. Pathological Dependence

Even healthy relationships involve some external regulation, including an exchange or trade of agency and a degree of dependency. When this remains moderate, enhances well-being, and allows individuals to have separate lives, it is called intimacy. In other situations, it becomes dependence, which can deteriorate into coercion, fantasy, and worse. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9-9]
