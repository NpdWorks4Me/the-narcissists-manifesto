# Impaired self-formation and identity diffusion

**Impaired self-formation** refers to a lifelong disruption in the development of a cohesive self, described in the source material in the context of cluster B personality disorders. People with these disorders are described as “selfless” in the sense that they lack a functional self or ego; rather than having a core identity, they exhibit what is called identity diffusion or identity disturbance. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## The self and its components

The source defines the self as having two components. The first is a sense of continuity — the felt sense that “this is me” across changes in circumstances, relationships, and environment. The second is the capacity to establish boundaries: knowing where the self stops and the world begins. Impaired self-formation involves a massive disruption of both components, because the child is not allowed to develop boundaries, separate from caregivers, explore the world, or grow. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## Etiology: nurture and developmental pathways

Formation of the self is presented as primarily shaped by nurture rather than nature. Studies cited in the source indicate that genetics or heredity contribute less than 5% of the variability in [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]], while the remaining 95% relates to the specific circumstances of the child’s upbringing, household, and family — “above all,” the source says, the child’s mother. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

Two developmental pathways are described. In the first, the child is cut off from reality. This can happen through idolization, pedestalization, adulation, and the repeated message that the child is perfect and never to blame. It also includes **instrumentalization**, where the child is used to fulfill the parent’s unfulfilled dreams and fantasies, and **parentification**, where the child is expected to act as the parent’s parent. In this pathway, love is conditional and performative: the child learns that being loved requires meeting standards and pleasing others. The child is also cut off from peer interaction and feedback, leading to impaired reality testing later in life. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

In the second, more classical pathway, the child experiences abuse or neglect: physical, verbal, psychological, or sexual abuse, adverse childhood experiences, or abandonment. In both pathways, the parent is not available as a parent, and the child makes desperate attempts to regain the parent. In both cases, the child is objectified and learns to self-objectify, becoming an extension or servant of the parent rather than a full human being. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## Mechanisms: objectification, performative love, and dissociation

Objectification by the parents is internalized and introjected by the child. The individual then proceeds through life self-objectifying and objectifying others; objectification becomes the only mode of relating to self and others. The child is punished for signs of separateness, personal autonomy, independence, or agency. The implicit parental message is: “I love you only if and when you don’t exist.” ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

To preserve the relationship with the parent, the child learns to suspend existence and become an emptiness or absence. One classical mechanism for this is dissociation: whereas healthy children learn to integrate, this child learns that integration, emergence, and having a self are dangerous because they threaten the parent and risk punishment or abandonment. Development is therefore stunted or arrested. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## Consequences

Because boundaries never form and the child is not allowed to become an individual, the result is a lifelong absence of a functioning ego. The source notes the irony that people with [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]] are “not egotists because they don’t have an ego”; instead, they have identity diffusion or identity disturbance. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]
