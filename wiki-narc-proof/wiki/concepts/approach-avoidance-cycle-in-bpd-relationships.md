# Approach-Avoidance Cycle in BPD Relationships

The approach-avoidance cycle describes a recurring relational pattern in couples where one partner has borderline personality disorder (BPD). In the cycle, the borderline partner approaches, intimacy is created, and then the borderline partner becomes afraid of being abandoned or rejected while simultaneously feeling taken over by the other person. This produces [[engulfment-anxiety|Engulfment Anxiety]], prompting withdrawal. After withdrawing, the borderline partner feels abandoned and anxious, and approaches again. The partner’s responses mirror and reinforce this back-and-forth dynamic. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]

Healthy partners of a borderline person often develop an internal object — a mental snapshot of the borderline partner that is reliable, caring, empathic, and non-dangerous. They interact with this internal image rather than with the real-life person, because interacting with the actual borderline partner requires what psychology calls "high-effort coping." This temporary, situational shift toward interacting with an internal representation rather than the external person is described as a narcissistic defense. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]

The borderline partner is hypervigilant, constantly monitoring for possible abandonment or rejection. She catastrophizes, over-interprets, and may mislabel normal behavior as abandonment. She senses the partner’s withdrawal and knows she is introducing chaos, but feels unable to stop because she is dysregulated. When she feels abandoned or rejected, her defenses crumble and she may decompensate, acting out aggressively, recklessly, or immorally. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]

In a desperate attempt to ensure [[object constancy]], the borderline partner may try to "freeze" the partner — to prevent him from evolving, growing, traveling, having friends, or paying attention to anyone else. This is similar to the narcissist’s freezing of a partner, but for different reasons: the borderline wants the partner to remain a stable, ever-present source of attention and regulation. This attempt to deny the partner agency can feel suffocating and provoke [[engulfment anxiety]] in the partner. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]

Partners pushed into this dynamic may begin to avoid the borderline, developing avoidant behaviors and anxiety about both the external object and the internal object — described as "introject anxiety." They are caught between the borderline’s need for constant presence and their own need for [[object constancy]]. By withdrawing into an internal image of the partner, even healthy partners can undermine their own object constancy and become transiently personality-disordered in their defenses. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]

The whole dynamic provokes the borderline’s [[abandonment anxiety]], while the partner develops [[engulfment-anxiety|Engulfment Anxiety]]. Each side’s approach and withdrawal feeds the other, producing the self-reinforcing approach-avoidance cycle typical of BPD relationships. ^[yt-vjdkwbh4fzy.md:9-9]
