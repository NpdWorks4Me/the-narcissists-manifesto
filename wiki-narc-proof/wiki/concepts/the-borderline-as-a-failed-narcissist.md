# The Borderline as a Failed Narcissist

The concept of the borderline as a failed narcissist is attributed to Grostein and discussed by Sam Batn in the context of borderline personality disorder, narcissism, and dissociation. The formulation holds that the borderline’s pathology did not progress or devolve into narcissism, which is understood as a full-fledged form of binary dissociative identity disorder with two selves: the false self and the true self. The borderline’s instability reflects this unfinished structure. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

In this account, the narcissist resolves the duality of selves by switching off the “dilapidated, atrophied and dysfunctional” true self and relegating it to the deepest recesses of the mind, where it has no influence on the narcissist’s psychodynamics. What remains is only the false self, and this false self is the narcissist. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

The borderline, by contrast, fails to repress and dissociate the true self. Because the true self is not eliminated, the borderline’s two selves compete for control of her identity and memories. This competition is absent in the narcissist, whose true self has been effectively removed. The “failure” of the borderline is therefore not a simple deficit but a structural difference in how the self-states are organized. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

The formulation is presented as a reversal of Otto Kernberg’s hierarchy. Kernberg suggested that borderlines are closer to psychosis than narcissists; the alternative view argues that the narcissist is closer to psychosis, with a less organized personality. Only rigid grandiosity keeps the narcissist together; when that grandiosity is effectively challenged, the narcissist decompensates, acts out, and disintegrates. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]

In the same discussion, borderline personality disorder is described as a subspecies of dissociative identity disorder, with mood liability and emotional dysregulation reflecting switching between a host personality and an alter personality. The “failed narcissist” formulation follows this discussion of dissociative self-states and helps explain why the borderline, unlike the narcissist, experiences ongoing conflict between competing selves. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9]
