## Overview

The [[International Classification of Diseases]] (ICD) and the [[Diagnostic and Statistical Manual]] (DSM) are described in the lecture as embodying radically different diagnostic philosophies. The lecturer states that the ICD dictates a treatment plan customized to each client: there is no such thing as a diagnosis in the usual sense. Instead, the clinician describes and analyzes the individual, then creates a treatment plan unique to that individual and not applicable to anyone else. ^[yt-hko6ujraz7m.md:9]

The DSM, by contrast, is portrayed as categorical and based on lists. Because it treats conditions as separate diagnoses, it implies different treatments for different categories—for example, a psychopath should be treated differently from a narcissist because these are two different diagnoses. The lecture characterizes this as a radical difference from the ICD philosophy. ^[yt-hko6ujraz7m.md:9]

## Diagnostic categories vs. a single underlying disorder

The lecture connects this philosophical split to the question of whether [[Cluster B personality disorders]] are separate conditions or facets of a single underlying disorder. Using the parable of the blind wise men describing an elephant, the lecturer suggests that different diagnostic labels may describe the same phenomenon from different perspectives. The lecturer reports having argued for 30 years that all [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]] are actually a single personality disorder, and therefore should be treated identically. ^[yt-hko6ujraz7m.md:9]

The ICD is described as adopting the same general approach: it treats the cluster B disorders as one disorder while still customizing treatment to the individual patient. The lecture claims the DSM committed a serious mistake and has misled scholars and clinicians for a long time. It also notes that clinicians working with cluster B patients commonly see the same client as a narcissist on Monday, a borderline on Wednesday, and a psychopath on Friday—evidence that the unifying clinical features are extensive and foundational. ^[yt-hko6ujraz7m.md:9]

## Relational definition and the status of mental illness

The lecture raises a broader debate about whether these conditions are clinical entities at all. The lecturer states that [[psychopathy]] is not considered a mental illness, and expresses grave doubts about whether [[narcissistic personality disorder]] is a mental disorder. One cited reason is that these disorders are defined relationally—by how a person relates to other people and society—unlike diseases such as tuberculosis, malaria, or cancer, which stand alone and do not depend on social context or interaction. This relational way of defining disorders is called a "very dubious proposition." ^[yt-hko6ujraz7m.md:9]
