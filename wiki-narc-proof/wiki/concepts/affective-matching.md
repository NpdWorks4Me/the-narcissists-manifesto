## Affective Matching

**Affective matching** is a psychological mechanism that explains how Shame, Guilt, and Remorse can sometimes lead to continued or increased abusive behavior rather than inhibiting it. In the source material, Sam Vaknin describes it as one of three mechanisms — alongside Alloplastic Defenses and Reaction Formation — that mediate the transition between these negative affects and externalized aggression. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9-9]

According to the source, affective matching occurs when a person who experiences shame, guilt, or remorse tries to justify and validate those emotions by behaving in ways that correspond to them. The person interprets the negative affect as defining who they are: “that's who I am,” “my true nature,” and “I'm going to be loyal to myself.” ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9-9]

The person then rejects any demand to change, framing change as self-betrayal. They continue the behavior that generated the negative affect, treating the shame, guilt, or remorse as the “cost of doing business” or “the price of being me.” The source says this is presented as a form of authenticity: “this is my essence,” “quiddity,” and “I'm an authentic person.” ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9-9]

Vaknin situates affective matching among people with dark personality features, including narcissistic and psychopathic personality styles, Cluster B Personality Disorders such as Borderline Personality Disorder, and subclinical Dark Triad/dark tetrad traits. In these contexts, negative affects do not act as brakes on misconduct; instead, they become disinhibitory and can fuel further abuse. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9-9]

Affective matching differs from the other two mechanisms described in the source: alloplastic defenses involve blaming the person who triggered the negative affect, while reaction formation involves handing the shame, guilt, or remorse over to someone else through projection and Projective Identification. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9-9]
