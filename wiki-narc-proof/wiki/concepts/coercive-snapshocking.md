# Coercive snapshocking

Coercive snapshocking is a term used in analyses of narcissistic aggression to describe the process by which a narcissist attempts to force another person to match the internal image the narcissist holds of them. The narcissist's aggression is intended to make the target "coalesce" or "merge" with the internal object in his mind that represents them. The goal is to make the target stop existing as a separate external person and instead fuse with the narcissist's mental representation of them, thereby eliminating the frustration caused by the mismatch. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

## Frustration and aggression

In mentally healthy people, frustration-induced aggression is meant to signal displeasure and modify the other party's behavior. In the narcissist, however, aggression is externalized and reckless, and often culminates in verbal or physical violence. This aggression is the core of coercive snapshocking: an attempt to force the target to be submissive, obedient, and compliant with the narcissist's expectations. If the target insists on independence, personal autonomy, agency, or walks away, the narcissist may escalate toward attempts to eradicate, obliterate, or coerce the person. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

The narcissist perceives frustration as coming from inside rather than from an external source. According to the source, narcissists are incapable of perceiving the separateness and externality of objects; they interact only with the internal object that represents the other person. Aggression is therefore an inside job — an attempt to reduce dissonance and anxiety by modifying the target into a compliant internal object. Walking away does not work, because the narcissist carries the internal object with them and continues to be frustrated by it from within. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

The source describes three possible ways the narcissist can try to resolve this internal frustration: psychologically, through brainwashing or indoctrination; physically, through violence; or by coercing the target to behave in a way that does not challenge the internal object. Until one of these solutions is achieved, the frustration persists because the target's "avatar" is attacking the narcissist from inside like a Trojan horse or fifth column. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

## Relation to self-states

Under stress, anxiety, frustration, humiliation, or narcissistic injury, the narcissist may transition into a [[narcissistic rage|borderline self-state]]. In this state, defenses shut down and the narcissist emotionally dysregulates. If the frustrating environment persists, the narcissist transitions to a primary psychopathic state. The borderline state is impulsive and destructive; the psychopathic state is cold, premeditated, ruthless, callous, relentless, and lacks empathy. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

There is also a covert phase that can act as a bridge between the borderline and psychopathic states. During this phase, the narcissist appears completely normal: seeking consensus, compromising, caring, or even loving, while also showing hints of sarcasm, bitterness, and passive aggression. Beneath this mask of sanity, the narcissist continues to plan the final act of the target's destruction — which may involve ruining a career, traumatizing the victim, or inflicting enormous damage. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

Two variants of the borderline self-state are described. In one, the narcissist suddenly erupts into a temper tantrum with no preceding calm. In the other, there is a gradual escalation: the narcissist tries to provoke a fight, needling the target through [[projective identification]] in order to create a situation where the victim can be blamed for the conflict. This escalatory variant is described as typical of covert narcissists. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

When the narcissist transitions to the psychopathic self-state, the conflict may appear resolved: consensus has been restored, peace has been declared, and the target feels safe. In fact, the narcissist is planning revenge and payback while performing normality. The source compares this deceptive phase to a situation where one party appears to return to normal while secretly preparing destruction. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

## Alloplastic defenses

The narcissist's aggression is supported by [[alloplastic defenses]], in which the narcissist blames other people for their own behavior and its consequences. The narcissist uses an [[external-locus-of-control|External Locus of Control]], claiming "You made me do it," and therefore frames their aggression as a justified response to victimization. This aggravates antisocial behavior, because the narcissist comes to see the conflict as a matter of survival: if he does not win, he will be eradicated. Winning becomes the sole priority. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

If the narcissist fails to win, the source describes a collapse into extreme depressive or dysphoric mood, often accompanied by substance abuse, withdrawal from reality, and disrupted daily or professional routines. The narcissist may disintegrate and gradually transition toward a prepsychotic stage. The source also notes that narcissists often use verbal and psychological abuse against those closest to them, because intimacy breeds aggression in the narcissist. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]
