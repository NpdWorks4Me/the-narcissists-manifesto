# Shame, Guilt, and Remorse as Drivers of Abuse

The video transcript argues that, contrary to expectation, **shame, guilt, and remorse can act as drivers of abuse** rather than brakes on it. It describes these emotions as normally inhibitory: they arise through socialization, become internalized as conscience, and usually cause people to stop misconduct. However, in people with pathological or subclinical personality traits, the same emotions can trigger aggression, acting out, and increased abuse. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

## Shame, guilt, and remorse in personality disorders

According to the source, the mainstream view is that narcissists and psychopaths lack remorse and guilt, but that narcissists do experience shame. This shame is described as a reservoir the narcissist spends a lifetime avoiding; when a narcissistic injury or mortification occurs, contact with this shame can feel life-threatening. Psychopaths, by contrast, are said to be incapable of shame. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

The transcript distinguishes between disorders. In borderline personality disorder, secondary psychopathy, and histrionic personality disorder, people are said to experience shame, guilt, and remorse after acting out. But in narcissistic personality disorder, malignant narcissism, antisocial personality disorder, and primary psychopathy, there is no trace of guilt or remorse. Defenses are in place that make the person impervious: they reframe events, justify their actions, insist they are never wrong, create self-validating narratives, and sometimes brag about egregious or criminal behavior. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

## Why these emotions can lead to abuse

Normally, shame, guilt, and remorse inhibit action; they are "brakes" on misconduct. They are social constructs transmitted through parents and other socializing agents, and they become the conscience. Yet when experienced by people with high narcissistic defenses, psychopathic or antisocial tendencies, dark triad or dark tetrad traits, narcissistic or psychopathic personality styles, or borderline features, these emotions can be disinhibitory and lead to externalized aggression. The source identifies three mechanisms, among many, that mediate this transition. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]

### Alloplastic defenses

The first mechanism is alloplastic defenses. When a disordered person feels shame, guilt, or remorse, they experience the emotion as ego-dystonic—uncomfortable, as though they no longer feel right in their own skin or as a "good person." Instead of accepting responsibility, they blame the person who made them feel that way. The victim need not have provoked them; their mere presence can be enough. For example, a person with borderline traits who acts out against an intimate partner may feel ashamed and guilty, but grandiosity and alloplastic defenses lead them to say, "You made me do it," "You guilt-tripped me," or "You triggered me." The anger is then externalized against the perceived source, and the abuse escalates as a way to punish or annihilate that source. ^[yt-xmowschonlc.md:9]
