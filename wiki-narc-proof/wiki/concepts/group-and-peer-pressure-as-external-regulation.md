## Group and Peer Pressure as [[external-regulation|External Regulation]]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is defined as the use of other people to regulate and stabilize one’s own emotions and moods. When a person is overwhelmed by emotions or experiences labile moods, they may turn to others to restore inner peace and harmony. This is contrasted with healthy self-regulation: most psychologically healthy people regulate from within, using internal mechanisms to stabilize fluctuating moods and emotions, and are largely self-sufficient in reaching equilibrium and homeostasis. In such people, emotions function as signals rather than threats. By contrast, emotion dysregulation and mood lability are key clinical features of conditions such as [[Borderline Personality Disorder]]. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

### Why [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] Is Appealing

The source asks why anyone would choose [[external-regulation|External Regulation]], even when [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]] is available, and why there is strong resistance to [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]] in therapeutic settings among people with borderline and narcissistic pathology. One answer lies in the tradeoff: [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] creates dependency and an external [[Locus of Control]], making the person feel controlled from the outside and unable to determine their own life. Yet this position carries significant advantages. When regulated externally, a person can blame others for failures and mistakes, thereby avoiding shame and guilt. [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is also described as anxiolytic: if someone else is in charge of one’s emotions and moods, the individual is not responsible, accountable, or at fault. This absence of accountability is described as an addictive sensation that many people find irresistible. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

A similar dynamic is observed in the relationship between authoritarian leaders and populations. The authoritarian leader offers a “Faustian deal”: the leader assumes full responsibility, so followers need only surrender their power. This relieves followers of anxiety, guilt, and shame, making [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] an integral part of compliance with dictatorships and authoritarian systems. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

### Groups, Peer Pressure, and [[external-regulation|External Regulation]]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is very common in collective settings. In the ingroup, regulation is carried out by the group itself. When a person joins a group or becomes assimilated into a collective, the group controls behaviors, cognitions, emotions, choices, and decisions. The locus of control resides in the group rather than the individual, producing what is described as a “cult mind” or “mob mind.” ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

Peer pressure is a specific form of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]]. Peers surround the individual, exert influence, communicate expectations, and punish non-conformity. Groups and peers therefore always involve [[external-regulation|External Regulation]], mediated through expectations and social control. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

### Narratives, Symbols, and Intimacy

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is also mediated via narratives and symbols. Examples include [[Morality Plays]] in which people split into “all good” and “all bad,” conspiracy theories, and religion, where control is assigned to God or to the forces of good against evil. When individuals manipulate symbols, they may relinquish control and relegate it to the symbolic realm, a theme associated with the work of Jacques Lacan. These narratives reduce anxiety and absolve the individual of responsibility, allowing integration into a group. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is further linked to intimacy. Intimacy involves exposing vulnerability, on the one hand, and handing over some power or control to another person, on the other. Intimacy can therefore be understood as a form of regulatory outsourcing. The source notes the irony that people with [[Narcissistic Personality Disorder]] are incapable of genuine intimacy; instead, they replace it with fantastic intimacy, living inside a shared fantasy. When the narcissist becomes embedded in such a shared fantasy, they become regulated from the outside through narcissistic supply and the fantasy itself. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

### Pathological Manifestations and Dependence

When [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] becomes intense and pervasive, it appears in pathological forms. In borderline personality disorder and dependent personality disorder, commonly called codependence, [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is carried out by another person or group. In intimate relationships, the partner becomes the outsourced and externalized ego, expected to manage the individual’s happiness, moods, emotions, and well-being. For the borderline, the partner is expected to regulate needs and expectations; for the codependent, the partner is expected to regulate the narcissist’s self-concept through self-enhancement or [[Narcissistic Supply]]. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] also occurs in healthy relationships. In intimacy, parties exchange and trade agency, creating some dependency. When this exchange remains limited, enhances well-being, and allows separate lives and self-exploration, it is called intimacy; when it goes too far, it becomes dependence, which can deteriorate into coercion, fantasy, and worse. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]
