# External Locus of Control

**External locus of control** describes the condition in which a person experiences their life as determined from outside rather than from within. In the framework of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] — the use of other people to stabilize one's own emotions and moods — the externally regulated individual develops an external locus of control: they are controlled from the outside, are not in charge of their own psychological states, and become dependent on an external regulator. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

## [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] and Its Counterpart

Most healthy, normal people regulate from the inside, relying on internal regulatory mechanisms to stabilize fluctuating moods and emotions. They are self-sufficient in this regard: they do not need the presence or contributions of other people to reach a state of equilibrium and homeostasis. Healthy people are stable around the average, without very high highs or very low lows; their emotions function as signals rather than threats. By contrast, emotion dysregulation is a key clinical feature of certain mental disorders, most notably [[Borderline Personality Disorder]], where it appears together with mood lability. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

When a person regulates from the outside, they become dependent on the external regulator. Control resides outside the self, and the individual is reduced, in the source's terms, to "a play thing" — an object rather than an agent. This experience is described as egodystonic and even menacing, yet many vulnerable people nonetheless choose [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] over [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]], even when [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]] is available — for example, in the wake of psychotherapy, where strong resistance to [[internal-regulation|Internal Regulation]] is observed in people with borderline and [[Narcissistic Personality Disorder]]. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

## The Appeal of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]]

The persistence of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is explained by tradeoffs that make it addictive and hard to resist. One advantage is the availability of alloplastic defenses: if a person is regulated from the outside, they can blame everything that happens to them on other people. Failures, defeats, wrong decisions, harm caused to others, inadequacy, or lack of knowledge can all be shifted onto someone else, thereby avoiding the negative effects of [[Shame]] and guilt. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

A second advantage is that [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] tends to reduce or mitigate [[Anxiety]]; it is anxiolytic. When someone else is in charge of one's emotions and moods, the individual is not responsible for anything and will not experience remorse, regret, shame, or guilt, because any adverse consequences are attributed to the external regulator. Being not in charge, not responsible, and not accountable is described as a very addictive sensation. This dynamic is held to explain why people choose or vote for dictators and authoritarian leaders: the authoritarian strikes a Faustian deal with the populace, taking all responsibility onto himself and offering freedom from anxiety, guilt, and shame in exchange for their power. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

## Groups, Narratives, and Symbols

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is very common in collective settings and groups. Within an ingroup, regulation is carried out by the group itself: when a person joins a group, assimilates into a collective, or becomes "an atom in the mob," they develop a cult mind or mob mind. The group then controls behaviors, cognitions, emotions, choices, and decisions, and the locus of control resides in the group. A similar process occurs in peer interactions: [[Peer Pressure]] is a form of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]], in which peers exert influence, communicate expectations, and punish nonconformity. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is mediated through narratives and symbols; it is a form of storytelling. Examples include morality plays, in which people split the world into "all good" and "all bad"; conspiracy theories; and religion, in which a person is controlled by God. In these narratives, individuals relinquish control and relegate it to the symbolic realm — a process associated with Jacques Lacan's work on the imaginary and the symbolic. By reducing anxiety and absolving the individual of responsibility and accountability, such narratives allow for integration into a group. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

## Intimacy and Regulatory Outsourcing

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is also described as a precondition for [[Intimacy]], because intimacy involves both the exposure or communication of vulnerability and the handing over of some power or control to another individual. In this view, intimacy is a form of regulatory outsourcing, present in dyads, groups, and collectives. This creates an irony: people with narcissistic personality disorder are actually incapable of the kind of intimacy normally recognized as such, and instead replace true intimacy with fantastic intimacy — an intimacy that exists only in the mind. Once the narcissist becomes embedded in a shared fantasy, however, they become regulated from the outside, via sources of narcissistic supply and the shared fantasy itself. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

## Pathological and Healthy Extremes

When [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is taken to extreme — too intense and too all-pervasive — it becomes a pathology, as in borderline personality disorder and dependent personality disorder (colloquially known as codependence). In interpersonal relationships, the intimate partner becomes the external regulator: the partner is placed in charge of one's happiness, well-being, moods, affections, and emotions, functioning as an outsourced, externalized ego. The expected regulatory role differs by pathology: in the borderline, the intimate partner is expected to regulate needs and expectations; in the codependent, to regulate needs; and in the case of the narcissist, the partner is expected to regulate the self-concept via self-enhancement or narcissistic supply. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]

[[external-regulation|External Regulation]] is thus multifaceted and manifests differently across pathologies. Even in healthy relationships, some amount of [[external-regulation|External Regulation]] and exchange of agency exists between partners, along with some emerging dependency. When this exchange does not go too far — when it enhances well-being and allows individuals to continue having separate lives and exploring themselves — it is called intimacy. In all other situations, it is dependence, which can devolve into coercion, fantasy, and worse. ^[yt-enjcx2uswho.md:9]
