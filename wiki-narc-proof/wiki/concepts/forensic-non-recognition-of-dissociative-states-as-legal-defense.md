## Forensic Non-Recognition of Dissociative States as Legal Defense

**Forensic non-recognition of dissociative states as legal defense** refers to the refusal of courts to accept dissociative identity disorder (DID) or borderline personality disorder (BPD) as a mitigating circumstance or criminal defense. According to the source transcript, courts worldwide reject these diagnoses as defenses, and defendants are imprisoned even when it is proven beyond doubt that the offense was committed while an alter personality, rather than the host personality, was in control. The stated basis for this position is the claim that it is a myth that people with BPD or DID cannot fully control the behaviors and choices of their alters; the source asserts that they can. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]

### Clinical background

Dissociative phenomena—including depersonalization, derealization, and dissociative amnesia—are described as common reactions in BPD and DID, formerly known as multiple personality disorder. Similar dissociative reactions are also noted in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD). Patients describe the experience as being on autopilot, sliding into anesthesia, or becoming a detached spectator. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]

Severe dissociation is a diagnostic criterion for BPD, listed as criterion number nine. The transcript further characterizes BPD as “a subspecies of dissociative identity disorder,” with mood liability and emotional dysregulation representing outward manifestations of switching between self-states rather than ordinary mood changes. In BPD, the dissociative trigger is typically actual abandonment or the perceived threat of rejection and separation from an intimate partner, resulting in intense abandonment or separation anxiety. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]

### Psychopathic self-states and criminal behavior

The source links dissociative states in BPD with the emergence of a secondary psychopathic self-state. When internal stress, guilt, shame, or anticipated abandonment becomes intolerable, the individual may hand over control to this inner psychopath, after which depersonalization, derealization, or amnesia may occur. The behavior that follows can be impulsive, aggressive, reckless, promiscuous, antisocial, and lacking in empathy. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]

Substance abuse is described as exacerbating these reactive patterns. The transcript notes that patients may misattribute their dissociative amnesia to alcohol or drugs, when in fact another self-state emerged. This pattern is forensically significant because it offers a clinical explanation for how dissociative states may accompany criminal conduct. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]

### Legal consequence

The core forensic consequence described in the source is that the legal system does not treat dissociative states as negating responsibility. Even when an alter personality was shown to be in control at the time of the crime, the courts are said to reject the diagnosis as a mitigating circumstance. The source’s conclusion is that such defendants are imprisoned despite proof of dissociative involvement. ^[yt-ev1gb3-de-8.md:9-9]
