# Approach-Avoidance Repetition Compulsion in Maternal Relationships

## Overview

Approach-avoidance repetition compulsion describes a pattern in maternal relationships in which a child "can never win." When the child gratifies the mother or meets her expectations and needs, the mother feels suffocated and threatened, because she believes she is then expected to reciprocate — something she is unable to do. At that moment she reverses course and renders the child a "persecutory object" or an enemy, challenging the child to be the cause of her failure as a mother. This cycle prevents the relationship from reaching a stable plateau of intimacy. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Dynamics

The mother communicates a persistent underlying message: "something's wrong with you." Other people love her, but the child does not; the child is incapable of making her feel good or happy; the child is constantly failing, disappointing, and inadequate. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

The child may occupy different roles at different times, such as the scapegoat or the golden child. These roles are described as transitory, meaning a child can move between them. Even when the mother does many things right and encourages the child, there are periods when nothing the child does can satiate her need for love and attention. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Effects on the Child

As a result of this pattern, the child can develop what is called an internalized "bad object" — a constellation of internal voices telling the child that they are unworthy, inadequate, a failure, ugly, stupid, or disappointing. These internalized voices are known as introjects. They may persist for life, undermining self-esteem and corroding the person's sense of accomplishment even after they appear to have recovered or become successful. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Clinical Context

The source suggests that when a mother's difficult behavior solidifies or intensifies with age, she is unlikely to be a "pure borderline." Instead, she may carry co-morbidities such as narcissistic personality disorder, histrionic personality disorder, or psychopathy. Borderline personality disorder can be thought of as an "invisibility cloak": once it is tossed aside, what remains is the underlying narcissism, histrionic traits, or psychopathy. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

Narcissism does not ameliorate with age; with the exception of antisocial behaviors, all features of narcissism tend to become worse. In cases of dementia, certain cluster B personality disorders can also become accentuated, including narcissism. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Psychopathy and the Charming Mother

The transcript distinguishes psychopathy from the stereotype of the hardened criminal. Psychopathy exists on a spectrum, and most psychopaths are functional, socially adaptive, and can even be "pillars of the community." Studies cited in the source suggest that psychopaths are overrepresented among CEOs and surgeons. Their defining features include a goal-oriented style, lack of empathy, and callous, reckless, unscrupulous, and immoral behavior. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

Superficial charm and glibness are key clinical features of psychopathy. In maternal relationships, a mother who is publicly charismatic and magnetic but privately abusive to her child may therefore be more accurately described as psychopathic than purely borderline. The child becomes confused because the mother's public presentation contradicts the private experience, making it hard for others to believe the child's account. Some learned estimates in the source suggest that about one in three people with borderline personality disorder may also meet criteria for antisocial personality disorder, the clinical name for psychopathy. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]
