# Environmental etiology of cluster B disorders

**Cluster B personality disorders**, also known as the erratic or dramatic personality disorders, are characterized by dramatic, erratic, unpredictable, uncertain, and highly impulsive behavior. The DSM groups four diagnoses in this cluster: narcissistic personality disorder, borderline personality disorder, antisocial personality disorder (whose extreme form is psychopathy), and histrionic personality disorder. There is active debate about whether these are genuine clinical entities, in part because the same individual often manifests criteria for different cluster B diagnoses at different times. This co-occurrence or co-morbidity is a major problem for the diagnostic manual: most people diagnosed with one personality disorder are also diagnosed with at least one other, often alongside a mood, substance use, or anxiety disorder. This has encouraged a shift from the categorical approach toward a dimensional approach to diagnosis. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

The etiological question is usually framed as nature versus nurture. A key distinction is made between traits and disorders. For example, narcissism is a trait: it is hereditary, genetic, and present in every human being as “healthy narcissism” that regulates self-esteem, self-confidence, and sense of self-worth. The trait, however, is not the disorder. The source reports that multiple studies over at least 80 years have found a dysfunctional household, abuse, or trauma in the background of every patient and client with a cluster B personality disorder. Nurture—the environment—is therefore described as the major etiological contribution. Heredity is said to contribute less than 5% to the variability of [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]], with 95% attributed to the highly specific circumstances of the child’s upbringing, household, and family, and, “above all,” the child’s mother. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## Two developmental pathways

The source describes two developmental trajectories that lead to the emergence of most traits and clinical features common in [[cluster-b-personality-disorders|Cluster B personality disorders]].

The first pathway occurs when the child is cut off from reality and not allowed to interact meaningfully with peers. This can happen through idolization, pedestalization, adulation, and the message that the child can do no wrong and is never to blame, with the child’s “perfection” reified. It also includes instrumentalization, in which the child is used to fulfill the parent’s unfulfilled dreams, wishes, and fantasies, becoming an extension of the parent. A related pattern is parentification or adultification, in which an immature or dysfunctional parent expects the child to act as the parent. In these situations, the child is not in touch with reality, leading to impaired reality testing later in life. Overprotective parents deprive the child of peer feedback crucial for early development, so the child cannot develop boundaries or separate from the parents. Love is conditional on performance—performative love—and the child learns to act, meet expectations, and please others while also believing itself superior and never responsible or accountable. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

The second pathway is more classically abusive: physical abuse, verbal abuse, psychological abuse, and sexual abuse, including incest, which is described as a common etiology in borderline personality disorder. These are adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). Neglect—being ignored, rejected, or abandoned—also belongs to this second trajectory. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

## Shared mechanisms

Although the two pathways differ in content, the child’s reaction is described as identical because in both cases the parent is not present as a parent, and the child makes desperate attempts to regain the parent. In both trajectories, the child is objectified: instrumentalized, parentified, or treated as an object in abuse. The child learns to self-objectify in order to gain intermittent love, becoming an extension or servant rather than a full human being. Objectification is internalized and introjected, and the individual later proceeds to self-objectify and objectify everyone else; objectification becomes the individual’s only mode of relating to self and others. ^[yt-jkybqlinfei.md:9]

Both pathways interfere with boundary formation. A functioning self requires two components: a sense of continuity (“this is me”) and the ability to tell where the self stops and the world begins, that is, boundaries. When a child is not allowed to develop boundaries, there is a lifelong disruption in self formation.
