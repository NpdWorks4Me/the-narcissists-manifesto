# Narcissism Worsens with Age

The phrase **"Narcissism Worsens with Age"** reflects a clinical observation that narcissistic traits generally do not soften over time. According to the discussed source, narcissism does not ameliorate or mitigate with age; with the exception of antisocial behaviors, all other features of narcissism become worse. This is contrasted with borderline personality disorder, whose features may soften, leaving narcissistic traits more visible. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Narcissism and borderline co-morbidity

When a mother’s difficult behavior intensifies as she ages, the source suggests she is likely not a “pure” borderline but rather someone with co-morbid conditions, especially narcissism. The borderline part may be shed or reduced while the narcissistic part remains and becomes glaringly obvious. The borderline condition is described as an “invisibility cloak”: when it is removed, what is left behind may be narcissism, histrionic personality disorder, or psychopathy. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Dementia and narcissism

The intensification of narcissism with age is also observed in dementia. In dementia, certain [[Cluster B personality disorders]] become much worse. For example, a person diagnosed with narcissistic personality disorder who later develops dementia may find their narcissism accentuated, becoming much more deleterious and egregious. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Psychopathy as a co-morbidity

[[Psychopathy]] exists on a spectrum. The source notes that the overwhelming majority of psychopaths have nothing to do with serial killing; many are functional, pro-social, and socially adaptive. Studies are cited showing higher prevalence of psychopaths among chief executive officers and surgeons than in the general population. The key difference from a healthy person is that psychopaths tend to be goal-oriented, lack empathy, and can be callous, reckless, unscrupulous, and immoral. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

Psychopathy is also a common co-morbidity with borderline personality disorder. The source estimates that about one in three people with borderline may also meet criteria for antisocial personality disorder, the clinical name for psychopathy. A key diagnostic feature of psychopathy is superficial charm and glibness, which appears to a much lesser extent in narcissism. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

## Effects on adult children

The source describes how a mother with these traits may convey an underlying message to her child: that something is wrong with the child, that the child is a failure, disappointing, or incapable of making her happy. These roles may be transitory, with the child sometimes the scapegoat and sometimes the golden child. A pattern of approach-avoidance repetition compulsion may emerge: when the child gratifies the mother, she feels threatened or suffocated, and then flips, rendering the child a persecutory object or enemy. The child can never win. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]

As a result, the child may develop an internalized bad object — a constellation of internal voices telling the child they are unworthy, inadequate, ugly, stupid, or disappointing. These internalized voices, called introjects, can be carried for life and may continue to undermine self-esteem and self-confidence even after the child has become accomplished or recovered. ^[yt-urqzjiu3li4.md:9]
