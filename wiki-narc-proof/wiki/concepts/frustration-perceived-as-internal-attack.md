# Frustration perceived as internal attack

In narcissistic dynamics, frustration is typically misperceived as an internal attack. According to the source, narcissists are incapable of perceiving external objects as fully separate; they interact mainly with an internal object that represents the other person. When someone frustrates the narcissist, the frustration is not registered as coming from the outside, but is misperceived as emanating from inside. The other person is dehumanized and objectified, becoming a "figment," an avatar, or an introject in the narcissist's mind. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

This perception has direct consequences. The narcissist experiences the frustrating person's internalized avatar as attacking him from within, "like a Trojan horse or fifth column." Because the frustrating object is internal, walking away or ending the interaction does not resolve the frustration. The narcissist carries the object in his mind, and it continues to frustrate him from the inside until he eliminates the source psychologically, through indoctrination or brainwashing; physically, through violence; or by coercing the person to behave in a way that no longer challenges the internal object. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

## Relation to aggression

The source states that frustration breeds aggression, but in narcissists the aggression is not intended to signal displeasure or modify another person's behavior as it is in healthier people. Instead, it is externalized and reckless, and can culminate in verbal or physical violence. This is described as "[[coercive-snapshocking|Coercive snapshocking]]": aggression intended to force the other person to conform to, and merge with, the internal object in the narcissist's mind. If the person refuses to be submissive or insists on independence, autonomy, and agency, the narcissist may attempt to eradicate, obliterate, or annihilate them, or coerce them into compliance. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

## State transitions

Narcissists are described as having a low frustration threshold. Under stress, anxiety, frustration, mortification, or narcissistic injury, their defenses can shut down and they may transition into a [[borderline state]], marked by emotional dysregulation and impulsive, destructive rages. If the frustrating environment persists, the narcissist may transition further into a [[psychopathic state]], described as cold, premeditated, ruthless, and callous. This transition often includes a covert phase in which the narcissist appears normal, caring, consensus-seeking, or even loving, while secretly planning revenge and the other person's destruction. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]

The source also links this dynamic to [[alloplastic defenses]]: the narcissist attributes his own motivations and behavior to others, adopts an [[external-locus-of-control|External Locus of Control]] ("you made me do it"), and frames the conflict as a matter of survival. If he fails to win, he may sink into depressive or dysphoric states, sometimes with substance abuse and withdrawal from reality. ^[yt-9me2rxhcyuq.md:9]
